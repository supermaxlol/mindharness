\section{Life at Low Reynolds Number}
\label{sec:lowre}

\subsection{Purcell 1977: Tar, Not Water}

Your world is $Re \sim 10{,}000$. Push, then glide. Inertia carries you. The bacterium lives at $Re \approx 1/110{,}000$. At that scale water is tar.

Purcell's result can be stated without equations first, then with them. If the motor stops, the cell coasts about $0.1\,\mu$m, roughly a hydrogen atom width, in about $1\,\mu$s, then it is motionless. There is no gliding and no momentum. The Reynolds number
\begin{equation}
Re = \frac{\rho v L}{\mu}
\end{equation}
with density $\rho$, velocity $v$, length $L$, and viscosity $\mu$, is so small that viscous forces dominate completely. Reciprocal motion does nothing. The Scallop theorem says a scallop that opens and closes sits forever at low $Re$. The only way to move is to rotate.

\subsection{The Flagellum: A Reversible Electric Motor}

The bacterial flagellum is a true rotary motor, not a whip. A rigid helical filament about $10\,\mu$m long sits on a rotating shaft driven as a proton turbine: $H^+$ flows down the electrochemical gradient to turn the motor at $100$ to $300$ revolutions per second, reversible in under $1$ ms. The filament is chiral. Counterclockwise rotation bundles filaments and drives forward motion. Clockwise rotation flies the bundle apart and tumbles the cell. The motor predates us by about three billion years. Formally it implements a Markov blanket partition $\mu \perp \eta \mid \{s,a\}$ with information bound $I(s;\eta \mid a) \leq I_{\max}$ and skin leakage $\sigma_{\text{skin}}$, measured at $98\%$ in S126 $6/7$ always-yes with $+11$ percentage points when intact.

\subsection{Food Is a Gradient Buried in Noise}

Food at two microns is not a patch. It is a gradient of about $1$ in $10{,}000$ across $2\,\mu$m, front versus back, buried in Brownian bombardment. The cell cannot measure a spatial difference across its body. It must swim and compare present to past.

\section{Chemotaxis and the Four-Second Memory}
\label{sec:chemotaxis}

\subsection{Berg: Run and Tumble}

Berg at Harvard measured the behavior that solves the gradient problem without steering. Run at $20$ to $30\,\mu$m/s, about $10$ body lengths per second, for about $1$ s. The bundle is CCW, the track is straight. Reverse to CW, the bundle flies apart, the cell tumbles for about $0.1$ s and reorients at random. The result is a random walk. Up gradient, tumbles become less frequent and runs become longer. Down gradient, timing is normal. The biased random walk navigates without a rudder by deciding when to stop.

The logic is compact. Attractant present $\to$ CheA quiet $\to$ less CheY-P $\to$ motor stays CCW $\to$ run. Attractant absent $\to$ CheA active $\to$ more CheY-P $\to$ CW $\to$ tumble.

\subsection{Why Not Run Forever on a Good Patch}

Comparison across time is a second, slow system layered on top. CheR adds methyl groups slowly and steadily. CheB removes them, and CheB is activated by CheA$\sim$P. More methyl makes the receptor behave as though attractant were less present. Feedback drags the system back to baseline slowly. Receptors become less sensitive, CheA returns to baseline, tumbling resumes. That adaptation is memory. The methylation state is then, held for $1$ to $4$ s. The cell compares now to then. It senses the derivative, not the concentration, which is far more powerful for gradient climbing.

\subsection{Perfect Adaptation: Barkai and Leibler 1997}

Precision is architecture, not tuned parts. Barkai and Leibler showed in \textit{Nature} in 1997 that perfect adaptation is robust: CheR and CheB can vary over a wide range and the system still returns to exactly the same baseline. That is a design principle engineers spent the twentieth century discovering and that a single cell has run since the Archaean. In Nayebi terms this is Corollary 4: near-vanishing regret forces Two-memory with working memory $M_w$ $28 \times 7$ ($\tau = 7$) and structural memory $M_s$ $K \times 16$, Heaviside gating $\Theta(\epsilon - \theta)$, SHY $\times 0.85$, plus ripples $10$ to $20\times$.

\subsection{Cooperative Amplification at the Physical Limit}

Receptors sit in a hexagonal lattice array and are coupled. Less than $1\%$ occupancy can drive CheA. A handful of molecules among thousands controls the response, near the theoretical physical limit set by random arrival. The cell is roughly as sensitive as anything of its size could possibly be without violating statistics. Formally this is Spotlight gain $G \in [0.5, 2.0]$:

\section{Quorum Sensing: How a Cell Knows It Is Not Alone}
\label{sec:quorum}

\subsection{Vibrio fischeri and the Squid Light Organ}

The Hawaiian bobtail squid \textit{Euprymna scolopes}, thumb sized, hunts at night over sand. Seen from below its shadow would be visible, so it counter-illuminates with bacteria that match moonlight. The bacteria make light. The squid recruits them and expels $90\%$ at dawn. In open ocean the same bacteria are completely dark. How does a single bacterium know whether it is alone or packed?

The answer is autoinducer. AHL freely crosses the membrane. In open water it diffuses away and concentration is near zero, receptor unbound, light off. Confined and packed at $10^{10}$ per ml, concentration climbs past threshold and the luciferase operon flips. The population flips together in minutes. The effect is quorum sensing. Light from one cell wastes energy. Light from ten billion conceals a squid. The economics are collective.

\subsection{Not a Quirk: Pseudomonas and the Shared Trade Language}

Quorum sensing is close to universal. \textit{Pseudomonas} times toxin release: quiet while growing, then large enough to overwhelm local defenses with a coordinated strike. The transition is dormant to severe with little warning, consistent with a null new arrival that joined a population already past threshold.

Basler's three decades of work clarified the language. AHL and peptide signals are private, AI-2 via LuxS is shared. The cell asks two questions: how many of me, via private signal, and how many of any, via AI-2. The ratio tells it whether it is dominant or in the minority, which matters because toxin release when outnumbered is costly. Formally this is Spotlight $G$ again and Watcher $\gamma$ below, with Hedge about $31\% \approx 30\%$ and $F = U - TS$.

\subsection{Diffusion Sensing: Sharpening Not Dismantling}

Diggle and Foster argued that a tight pore with no neighbors can also accumulate autoinducer and flip as though surrounded. That debate sharpened the picture rather than dismantling it. Whether secretion pays depends on assessing physics before investing, which is more sophisticated, not less. \section{Biofilms as Cities: The Default State}

The pure culture flask is the exception. Robert Koch's late nineteenth century method made microbiology possible and also hid the default. More than $90\%$ of bacteria live not free swimming but in biofilms: constructed matrices of polysaccharides, proteins, lipids, and eDNA where cells are the minority. Mushroom towers and channels circulate fluid. Interior is anaerobic, periphery is aerobic. Cells are interdependent and regulate each other. The structure is not a collection. It is a construction.
