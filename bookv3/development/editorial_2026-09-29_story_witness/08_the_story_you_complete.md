# Chapter 8: The Story You Complete

The lion hears an ox and is afraid.

He has never heard that sound. Its maker could be harmless, or it could be stronger than he is. He cannot tell, and he cannot ask without letting his court see that the king is frightened. So he stays in place. The other animals notice. One of them, a jackal named Dimnah, sees an opening.

Dimnah wants a position near the lion. His companion Kalīlah thinks they have enough to eat and should leave rulers alone. To make his point, Kalīlah tells him about a monkey who watched a carpenter split a log. The carpenter stepped away. The monkey climbed onto the log, pulled out the wedge, and was caught when the wood snapped shut. Here, inside the lion's story, is another story: an animal ruined by interfering in a craft he did not understand.

Dimnah hears the warning. He goes to the lion anyway. He believes he can read the king's fear from his behavior and turn useful advice into access. At first he helps resolve the mystery of the ox. Later, after the ox becomes the lion's friend, Dimnah begins working to separate them. He supplies the lion with a new account of the bull. The lion kills the bull.

The bull's death begins with a sound. Between the sound and the killing lies an account supplied by someone who benefits from being believed. The lion could have learned more about his friend. Instead, he lets an interested adviser stand between what happened and what it means. The fable makes the cost of that shortcut visible in one body on the ground.

This is the tale of the lion and the bull in *Kalīlah wa-Dimnah*, an animal-story collection carried through languages and revised across centuries. It is also a miniature of this book's argument. An event happens; somebody describes it; another mind completes the account; an action follows; somebody pays. The ledger has crossed from an external mark into the reader of the mark.

### The marks cannot read themselves

Imagine handing a page of Chinese writing to two people, one who reads it and one who does not. The marks are the same. One reader can use a learned language to recover what the sentence says; the other sees a pattern of strokes. This is a difference in training, not intelligence. Years of practice have built links between marks and language in one reader that the other lacks. No trick can pour the page's meaning into a brain without those links.

Now imagine two fluent readers. Both know what the sentences say. One was recently betrayed by a trusted adviser; the other has seen rulers dismiss honest warnings as plots. The first may leave Dimnah's court primed to suspect a colleague. The second may leave more afraid of a leader who mistakes suspicion for proof. They decoded the same marks. They did not complete the same story.

We need three separate entries in the account. The **manifest record** is what can be pointed to on the page: the lion pauses, Dimnah seeks access, the bull dies. The **evoked experience** is what a reader constructs while reading: a picture of the court, guesses about motives, a remembered argument, dread for the bull. The **retained model** is what remains available afterward and may guide a later decision. A book can carry the first entry across centuries. It can invite the second. It cannot guarantee the third, or dictate what the reader will do with it.

Meaning lives neither entirely in the ink nor entirely in an unconstrained reader. A written language and a plot impose limits. The ox cannot be made the animal who kills the lion without changing this tale. But the tale does not contain a sealed packet labeled *your future judgment*. The reader brings a lifetime to the encounter, and that lifetime changes which part of the story becomes useful.

The fable takes advantage of a different kind of training you already have. You know what it is to hear a sound you cannot place, to hide embarrassment, or to watch someone offer an explanation at a convenient moment. The story puts these familiar experiences in a new arrangement: a lion, an ox, two jackals. Its short surface can make a complex relation available for use in a court, an office, or a friendship. That portability is what the research notes call **relational compression**. The phrase is a label for the reading we just performed, not a substitute for it.

Kalīlah's monkey is a good test. Its surface lesson seems simple: stay out of work you do not understand. If Dimnah had followed it, the bull might have lived. A reader could stop there and call the tale a sermon against ambition.

But Dimnah offers the opposite argument. He says a place at court is won by effort, patience, and skill with powerful people. The lion really does have a problem that an adviser could help solve. The ambitious jackal is not wrong merely because he is ambitious. A court that treated every outsider as a meddling monkey would shut out capable counsel along with dangerous flatterers.

The two stories lodged inside the same episode compete. One protects people against reckless interference; the other protects them against timid surrender. Neither tells the king how to distinguish sound advice from a bid for control. That is the unsolved problem that moves the plot. Dimnah first earns trust by solving a real uncertainty. Once trusted, he uses the same position to supply a more dangerous interpretation. His first success helps make his later account credible.

A maxim would be easier to remember: *Beware the flatterer.* The tale is harder to forget because it gives the maxim a working environment. The ruler has a reputation to protect. The adviser has an incentive to be near him. The newcomer has a friendship the adviser can lose. The warning arrives after the adviser has already been useful. By the time you ask whether Dimnah is lying, you have lived through a reason the lion might listen.

Picture a workplace where a manager hears that a valued colleague is disloyal. The messenger has helped the manager before, and stands to gain if the colleague falls. You do not need jackals in the office to recognize the relation. A small cast and a few events hold a complicated pattern of dependence, ambition, trust, and verification. You can carry the pattern into settings the original tellers never saw.

The physical anchor is a folded map. A good map lets you carry miles of terrain in one pocket. When unfolded against a real road, it helps you choose a turn. The fable folds a social problem into a short sequence of characters and consequences. The reader unfolds it against a later life.

Here the map metaphor stops. Roads stay where the mapmaker drew them long enough for you to compare line with pavement. A human situation changes while you interpret it. The colleague may be innocent; the messenger may be honestly alarmed; you may be the one with something to gain from believing the accusation. The fable is a portable model of a possible relation, not evidence that the people in front of you occupy its roles.

The fable itself is an external ledger of that failure. It does not prove that these animals lived; it preserves a relation for later readers to try against their own encounters with power.

### What the experiments can see

It is tempting to say that a fable “rewires the brain” and leave it there. That sentence hides several questions: what a reader does during a story, what remains afterward, and whether the residue changes conduct outside the reading situation. The studies measure different parts of that route.

In a brain-imaging experiment, people reading narratives recruited systems involved in processing the kinds of actions and perceptions the sentences described. That is a clue about the work readers perform. They are building changing scenes, not merely identifying marks. It does not tell us whether a reader of *Kalīlah wa-Dimnah* later makes a wiser appointment at work. It helps explain how the court can become more than a list of names.

In another study, people read a novel over several evenings and underwent brain scans during and after the reading period. Their resting connectivity changed across days of reading and for several days afterward. The result gives the evoked experience a measurable afterlife. Connectivity is not a photograph of an installed moral rule. It does not tell us what belief each participant carried home or whether it survived a month.

Experiments that vary argument strength in stories find shifts in readers' expressed attitudes under the tested conditions. Stronger arguments generally persuaded more, especially under some combinations of prior doubt and immersion in the narrative. That result refuses an easy account of storytelling as a spell that slips past reason. What the story *argues* can still matter to readers caught up in it.

These findings line up without being interchangeable. One study observes the simulation of events while reading. Another observes a temporary change after reading. A third measures attitudes. They do not add up to a scan of the exact neural track laid by one ancient fable in one child's brain. They show why the page-to-person route deserves to be treated as a mechanism with distinct stages rather than dismissed as a metaphor.

### Why this record kept traveling

The fable did something the bull could not: it survived its first teller. Material associated with the Sanskrit *Panchatantra* passed through translation and adaptation. Ibn al-Muqaffaʿ made an Arabic *Kalīlah wa-Dimnah* in the eighth century. Later makers copied, illustrated, revised, and translated it again. Its texts were never a single frozen set of sentences. People could add a tale, recast a warning, or carry an image into another court.

Each copy was an act of selection. A scribe preserved some lines. A translator chose how a jackal would sound in a new language. A parent decided which part to tell a child. A reader remembered the bull and forgot the monkey. That filtering is part of the ledger's history. A record lasts through maintenance by many people, and their decisions alter what later people inherit.

There is an experimental neighbor to this history. Simon Kirby and colleagues passed artificial communication systems through chains of human learners. They found that pressure to make a system learnable favors compression, while pressure to communicate distinctions favors expressive structure. Put simply, if the next person cannot learn a code, it dies; if it cannot say what speakers need to say, they change it. Across transmissions, a usable system takes shape under both demands.

Kirby's participants did not transmit *Kalīlah wa-Dimnah*. The experiment cannot tell us which medieval manuscript wording survived because it was most memorable. It reveals a selection pressure that the tale's long journey makes vivid: a cultural object must be learnable enough to pass on and useful enough for people to keep passing it on. The lion, the bull, and the ambitious adviser are portable partly because readers can rebuild their relation without inheriting the original court.

Yet portability is also the risk. A story that can fit many situations can fit too many. Once a tale becomes a convenient explanation, it may outrun the evidence in any particular case. This is why stories are instruments for both teaching and propaganda. The same ability to make a relation instantly visible can make a weak resemblance feel decisive.

### Who owns the conclusion?

Let the rival explanations face the lion's court.

Perhaps it is *only entertainment*. Then it should make no difference to a reader's expectations once the page is closed. But readers simulate events while reading, some narrative encounters have measurable aftereffects, and experiments find that story features and arguments can move stated attitudes. Entertainment is one route by which the account is rehearsed; the word *only* does the evasion.

Perhaps the moral is *inside the text*, the same for everyone who can read it. Then Kalīlah's warning and Dimnah's defense of ambition should resolve cleanly into one instruction, and readers with different histories should draw the same practical inference. They do not. The record constrains the plot; it does not settle every use of the plot.

Perhaps the reader *creates the lesson alone*. Then the inherited cast and sequence are dispensable. But they are precisely what make the relation available to a person who has never met a lion's court. Change Dimnah into an adviser who loses by giving good counsel and you change the reader's problem. The marks matter because they direct attention, even when the reader can resist the direction.

Perhaps stories are *mind control*. Then whoever writes one should be able to fix the reader's later choice. The competing lessons inside this tale, the measured role of argument strength, and the range of lives readers bring to it defeat that clean control. A writer can influence what a reader imagines and remembers. A writer cannot know every world in which that reader will use the story.

One explanation survives those failures: the recorded tale and the reader's history work together to make a model available. Later, a decision can be steered by that model. Its value depends on whether the person can compare the remembered relation with the present evidence, especially when acting on the model would charge a cost to someone else.

That last condition is the trap in ordinary clothes. If you defend the lion because he trusted an adviser who had been useful, you must also defend a bank, official, or machine that treats a plausible account as a checked record. If you condemn the lion for killing on one untested report, you must ask the same of your own most vivid stories. The standard you apply to the ruler has followed you out of the fable.

The lion has heard a real sound. Dimnah has seen a real pause. Neither fact tells the court what the bull intends. The missing act is a check that does not pass through Dimnah. The lion lets one interested interpreter supply both the account and its meaning. The ox bears the cost.

When a story makes a real person look like Dimnah, name the observation before the character: *What did I actually see? Who told me? What would I expect to find if this person were innocent?* Then seek a source the interested messenger does not control. The aim is to use the fable to notice a danger while refusing to make resemblance itself a verdict.

For centuries, people supplied that second check imperfectly: ask a witness, consult a different account, wait before punishment, compare a story with what happened next. Now we ask machines to do some of this work at speed. Their screens can tell us that a record was reviewed. Their reports can say *I checked*. But that is another account a reader can believe. What exactly did the checker read before the light turned green?
