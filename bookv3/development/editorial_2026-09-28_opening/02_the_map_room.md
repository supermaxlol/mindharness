# Chapter 2: The Map Room

The point of the bone tool was still imaginable long after the last one vanished.

At sites in Tasmania, archaeologists found worked bone in older layers. In the later layers, the tools became less common, less varied, and then absent. At Rocky Cape, Joseph Henrich reported a ratio of stone to bone tools of three to one about seven thousand years ago. Several millennia later it was fifteen to one. By roughly three and a half thousand years ago, bone tools no longer appear in that record.

Imagine the last child who watched one being made. She sees a bone split, a point ground, a hand testing the edge. We do not know that child, her teacher, or the reason the practice ended. The scene is an imagined way of holding the archaeological problem. A lost object leaves no written account of what its makers thought they were losing.

One account of the disappearance begins with a rising sea. Tasmania had been connected to mainland Australia. As the sea filled the Bass Strait, the island's people became more isolated from other groups. Henrich proposed that the resulting reduction in the *effective* pool of people from whom one could learn made some techniques harder to maintain. If a difficult procedure is copied imperfectly, the best model available to the next learner matters. With enough models and enough correction, an occasional mistake need not become the next generation's starting point. With fewer, a useful distinction can gradually disappear.

This is more unsettling than a sudden disaster. A tool can be lost while every maker remains a capable human being. No one has to decide to destroy the knowledge. The next apprentice merely inherits a slightly poorer demonstration, then gives a slightly poorer demonstration in turn.

There is an obvious answer: perhaps people no longer needed that particular tool. Need changes with food, climate, materials, and other inventions. A lost artifact is not proof of a lost mind. Henrich's model can show how reduced contact *could* erode a technique; it cannot tell us by itself that this is why any one bone point ceased to be made. Archaeologists Krist Vaesen, Mark Collard, Richard Cosgrove, and Wil Roebroeks pressed the case much harder. They pointed to Tasmanian practices, including canoes, baskets, shelters, stories, and ritual knowledge, that continued or developed alongside the missing bone points. The headline story that an entire people became less culturally capable fails if it counts a vanished point while leaving all that surviving work out of the record.

Let the objection land. It improves the question. We do not need Tasmania to prove that every small community must lose its tools. We need to understand what makes a *particular* useful instruction reach the next person intact. Population matters only through the contact, selection, demonstration, materials, and correction that a learner can actually use. A census of people is a poor substitute for a map of those routes.

### Two maps of one city

In Debrecen, Hungary, researchers walked through sixteen city sections to study Hooded Crows. Over three years they completed 241 surveys and recorded 7,144 crow observations. The city offered an apparently simple map: put a dot where the crows are, count the trash bins, and find the places they favor.

The dots told two different stories. During the breeding season, more trash bins in a wider surrounding area went with more observed crows. Food drew birds in. Yet the estimated probability that a section would be newly occupied for breeding was *lower* where there were more trash bins and restaurants. The researchers suggested that sites attractive for foraging may be too disturbed for nesting; they also offered other possibilities, including the presence of young or nonbreeding birds. The data did not let them choose finally among those explanations.

The bird at the bin and the bird settling a nest may be the same species in the same city. Counting visits answers one question; tracking whether a place becomes a breeding site answers another. If you mistake the first map for the second, the evidence seems to contradict itself.

The crow study did not test whether crows taught one another anything. Its value here is as an allegory of a measurement mistake. In a human community, counting how many potential teachers are nearby does not yet tell us whether an apprentice can find the right one, watch the decisive motion, remember it, and return when the first attempt fails. The route by which a practice is used can differ from the route by which it survives.

The distinction becomes visible when people, rather than crows, are asked to pass something on.

### The paper plane that would not improve

Take a sheet of paper and try to make it fly as far as possible. At the next table, place the planes made by an earlier group. A new participant can study one, two, or four examples before making a plane for the next group. Which arrangement should produce the longest flight after several rounds?

The easy answer is four. More examples mean more designs and a better chance of encountering an excellent one. In a study of 543 participants, that first part happened: larger sets offered more variation and better available planes. But the expected inheritance did not follow. Plane distance improved across experimental generations in the condition where each participant saw one predecessor's plane. It did not improve across generations in the conditions with two or four models.

The experimenters examined the copies. Participants did favor better planes, yet the resemblance between one generation's planes and the next weakened as the number of models grew. More to inspect had not made the useful design easier to transmit. The investigators proposed limited working memory and the burden of comparing multiple designs as part of the explanation. The experiment does not say that one teacher is always better than four. It shows where the simple demographic rule can fail: additional examples increase the supply of possible knowledge without ensuring that any one example is learned faithfully enough to carry forward.

Now imagine a crowded workshop that gives every apprentice the best-looking tool but never shows which maker's version actually works. The room is full. The usable map is thin. A learner may borrow a feature from each design and make a worse hybrid. Or she may be unable to recall the one fold that mattered.

Another experiment complicated the answer in the opposite direction. Groups of six people tried to discover combinations of virtual ingredients. Some groups could see everybody else's attempts after every round. Others worked mostly in pairs, with occasional movement between pairs. The fully connected groups quickly learned from visible success, but tended to converge on one line of search. The partly connected groups kept different lines alive long enough to combine them. More than half of those groups discovered both prerequisite paths; none of the fully connected groups did.

The lesson is not that isolation is good. A sealed pair can lose a discovery as easily as a sealed island. The productive arrangement in that experiment combined local room to explore with later contact across groups. A useful cultural record needs both readable inheritance and a way to bring back a correction from somewhere else. Too little contact can strand knowledge. Too much imitation of the first visible winner can erase alternatives before anyone knows which will be needed.

Return to the child with the bone tool. How many makers existed is one question. Which makers she could watch, which variants survived, whether she knew a result was good, and whether she could try again are different questions. The map of transmission is drawn by those connections, not by a head count alone.

### The room around the mind

Take the map room away altogether. Leave a human newborn alone in a forest, with no caregiver, language, tools, or demonstration. The child would almost certainly die. Even if someone kept the child alive without bringing her into a human learning community, she would not invent speech, fire, and agriculture from a large brain in isolation. This is a counterfactual, not a report of an experiment on a “feral child.” An ape infant also needs its mother and group. The question is what each developing animal can learn in the social world available to it.

In a comparative test, researchers gave two-and-a-half-year-old children, chimpanzees, and orangutans tasks involving the physical and social world. The children and chimpanzees performed similarly on the physical tasks; the children did much better on the social ones. Human biological inheritance includes a remarkable capacity to learn *with* other people. It is not a finished library of human techniques waiting to unfold without them. The isolated-child image strips away the library; it does not measure a real abandoned child's intelligence or establish an overall ranking of species.

An experienced driver does something strange without noticing it. The car is wider than her body, yet she approaches a narrow gap and says, “I cannot fit.” She is judging the gap through a vehicle she has learned to handle. For the duration of the drive, her practical range of movement includes the steering wheel, the car's edges, and the road she can see. Her skin has not expanded to the doors. Her working ability has expanded into a system beyond her skin.

The same thing happens when a student learns to work with a map, a diagram, or mathematical notation. Give two children the same problem. Give one a familiar diagram, a worked example, and a teacher who can show where a calculation went wrong. Give the other the question alone. If the first child succeeds, the child has done the thinking. The marks around her have changed which thoughts she can carry to the end.

The driving image has a limit. A car usually extends one driver's action in the present. A shared notation can extend the action of strangers who lived centuries apart. It can also be taught selectively, guarded, revised, or imposed. The student does not simply *have* the map; she enters a room where other people have decided which maps remain on the shelves and how to read them.

This is what an external memory ledger adds to a capable brain. It gives a person a place to start that previous people paid to reach. The marks do not contain meaning by themselves. As with the clay tablet in the previous chapter, the reader needs the learned code and the circumstances of use. But a readable record can preserve a distinction after its maker dies. An error can be marked in the margin. Two inherited solutions can be set side by side and tested.

The popular account of genius credits the solitary navigator for reaching a destination. Sometimes a navigator really does make a rare turn. The map room asks a second question: which routes could she see that were invisible to someone else? A mathematician's notation, a craftsperson's demonstration, a scientist's failed trial, and a community's argument over a result all enter the next mind as possible moves. Originality is often a new route through inherited terrain.

Michael Muthukrishna, Joseph Henrich, and their colleagues carried the feedback further in the Cultural Brain Hypothesis. They modeled how access to socially learned information, the cost of maintaining a large brain, opportunities to learn from others, group structure, and the long period of human development can evolve together. A brain can make cultural learning possible; a growing store of useful culture can, in turn, change the value of a brain that can learn from it. The record shapes the organ that reads the record.

That is a model of a possible evolutionary route, tested against existing patterns, not a time-lapse film of human evolution. It closes a logical gap the solitary-genius story leaves open. If accumulated knowledge changes what a learner can do and what kinds of learners fare well, the biological mind and its cultural surroundings cannot be told as two independent success stories. The child inherits a brain and a world arranged for that brain to learn in. Each can alter the future of the other.

### Who keeps the maps?

At first, the map room sounds like a public gift. It can be. A correction attached to a recipe may save the next baker a failed loaf. A proof can let a stranger test a conclusion rather than trust the author's name. The room becomes more powerful each time an error can be found and a better version can reach the next learner.

But a map room is never a room of all possible maps. Someone chooses which book to copy, which language to teach, which maker's design to display, and which failure to leave out. A ledger can preserve a useful route and preserve a distortion with the same mechanical fidelity. Access determines who gets to correct the distortion before it becomes an inherited rule.

This is the more exact answer to the Tasmanian problem. A vanished bone point does not establish a verdict on a people's intelligence. A thriving craft elsewhere does not mean the missing point could be reconstructed at no cost. What matters is whether a particular practice has a maintained path from an earlier success to a later attempt, with enough comparison and feedback for a mistake to be repaired. When that path breaks, someone later pays the cost of discovery again. When the path holds, later people can spend their effort beyond the old limit.

The same architecture has a darker use. Suppose the map no longer tells a learner how to make a tool. Suppose it tells a court who owns the tool, a lender who owes the money, and a worker which company must answer for the loss. The people who keep that record can still decide which distinctions survive and which corrections arrive in time. Now the next reader's mistake is measured in cash, property, and claims against a company that may have been emptied before the claim is heard.

The map room made us powerful. The next question is who gets to own the map—and who receives the bill when its lines are drawn to someone else's advantage.

- Joseph Henrich, [“Demography and Cultural Evolution: How Adaptive Cultural Processes Can Produce Maladaptive Losses—The Tasmanian Case”](https://coevolution.fas.harvard.edu/sites/g/files/omnuum5841/files/culture_cognition_coevol_lab/files/henrich_2004-2.pdf), *American Antiquity* 69(2), 2004, describes the changing bone-tool record and models how an effective pool of social learners can affect transmission. - Krist Vaesen, Mark Collard, Richard Cosgrove, and Wil Roebroeks, [“Population Size Does Not Explain Past Changes in Cultural Complexity”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4843435/), *PNAS* 113(16), 2016, challenge the interpretation of Tasmania as an overall loss of cultural complexity and the sufficiency of population size as an explanation.
- Petra Paládi and colleagues, [“Spatial and Temporal Dynamics in the Use of Urban Habitats by Hooded Crows”](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41598-026-40561-z), *Scientific Reports* (2026), report 241 surveys of 16 sections and 7,144 observations; their foraging and breeding measures motivate the two-map allegory. The study did not measure social transmission.
- Nicolas Fay and colleagues, [“Increasing Population Size Can Inhibit Cumulative Cultural Evolution”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452720/), *PNAS* 116(14), 2019, report the 543-participant paper-plane transmission study, including the improved pool of examples and reduced copying fidelity. [Derex and colleagues' earlier group-size experiment](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12774) found benefits in a different task.
- Maxime Derex and colleagues, [“Partial Connectivity Increases Cultural Accumulation within Groups”](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4801235/), *PNAS* 113(11), 2016, report the six-person virtual-ingredient study and the advantage of preserving separate search paths with some later exchange.
- Michael Muthukrishna, Michael Doebeli, Maciej Chudek, and Joseph Henrich, [“The Cultural Brain Hypothesis”](https://journals.plos.org/ploscompbiol/article?id=10.1371/journal.pcbi.1006504), *PLOS Computational Biology* 14(11), 2018, model feedback among knowledge, learning, group structure, life history, and brain size. The skilled-driver image is adapted from the author's worldview notes on tools and the practical self.
- Esther Herrmann, Josep Call, María Victoria Hernández-Lloreda, Brian Hare, and Michael Tomasello, [“Humans Have Evolved Specialized Skills of Social Cognition”](https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/17823346/), *Science* 317, 2007, compare children, chimpanzees, and orangutans on physical and social cognition tasks. [Comparative work on human altriciality and brain development](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41559-023-02253-z) grounds the newborn's dependence. The forest scene is a thought experiment; accounts of alleged children raised by animals are not used as evidence for its outcome.
