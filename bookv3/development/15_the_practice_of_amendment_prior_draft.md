# Chapter 15: The Practice of Amendment

On Monday you promise yourself three hours of uninterrupted work. By ten o'clock you have answered messages, checked the news, and opened the file twice. You write *I failed again*. On Tuesday the same file feels heavier because it now carries Monday's verdict as well as Tuesday's work.

Here is a different experiment. You write, before the day begins: *After tea, at my desk, I will put the phone outside the room and mark one paragraph that needs revision. I will work for two minutes. Then I may stop.* At ten o'clock you do it. You may still have a difficult project. You have also learned something the earlier promise could not teach you: given a particular cue, room, and first action, you can begin.

This is the practice of amendment. You do not try to talk an old verdict out of existence. You build a record that can test it, and you arrange for the result to change the next attempt. The working notes use three old terms for parts of this move: **niyyah**, an intention aligned with a value; **muraqabah**, attentive observation of what is arising; and **hawa**, an impulse that may pull against the endorsed direction. They name practical distinctions here, not three newly discovered brain modules.

## First, find the right scale

An urge occurs in a moment. A routine grows across days. A story about who you are can last years. Use the moment to notice; use the day to practice; use the record to revise the story over time. Most plans fail because they try to solve all three scales with a command issued at the most exhausted point of the day.

Ask one prior question: *What is this task for?* “Become a disciplined person” is too broad to test. “Finish the proposal because I want to contribute to this work” names a direction. The direction can stay large; the next physical action should be small enough to observe.

Do not call the small action the whole ambition. Two minutes of revision are a doorway into a project, not the project itself. You may stop after two minutes, but once a week you should also inspect whether the doorway leads anywhere. A perfect record of opening files while the proposal never advances is a clue to redesign the plan.

## Before the attempt: make the next action easier to see

Write an **if–then plan**: *If I finish tea and sit at the desk, then I will put the phone in the kitchen and mark one paragraph for two minutes.* A recognizable cue removes one decision. A visible action tells you afterward whether it happened. The time limit makes beginning less expensive. Work on implementation intentions finds that such cue-linked plans improve goal attainment on average; the exact tea-and-paragraph ritual is yours to test.

Change one piece of the environment at the same time. Put the needed page on the desk. Leave the phone elsewhere. Close the feed. Ask for a quiet interval. This is not a contest to see how much temptation you can endure. It is a way to make the action you endorse easier to find when the urge arrives.

Check the biological baseline before treating a bad start as a character verdict. If you have slept badly for a week, missed meals, or worked through pain, the most useful next move may be rest, food, a shorter task, or support. A two-minute promise should never become a device for denying a real limit.

## During the attempt: treat the signal as information

You reach for the phone. Say, without drama, *I feel an urge to leave this.* The urge tells you something is happening; it does not settle what ought to happen. You may choose to continue for the planned two minutes. You may also discover that the obstacle is a missing instruction, a task too large to start, or a legitimate need to stop. In each case you have learned more than the sentence *I am lazy* could tell you.

The pause is useful because it separates sensation from verdict. A person can feel resistance and still mark the paragraph. That is a small instance of delayed gratification: a presently attractive option does not get the final vote merely because it arrived first. The pause is not a magical 200-millisecond executive switch. It is an opportunity, sometimes brief and sometimes longer, to bring an endorsed aim back into view. The action you repeat is part of what tomorrow's mind will have to work with; leaving the default unexamined also lets a pattern repeat.

When the two minutes end, record the action in ordinary language. *I marked the second paragraph and identified the missing source.* If you continued, say so. If you stopped, say so. Neither outcome needs a ceremonial compliment or an accusation.

## After the attempt: keep a ledger you actually use

Use a dated note with four lines:

> **I expected:** I would avoid the file once I felt resistance. 
> **I planned:** After tea, move the phone and mark one paragraph for two minutes. 
> **What happened:** I began, marked a paragraph, and stopped after four minutes. 
> **Next amendment:** Tomorrow, put the source document beside the file before tea.

The prediction line matters. Without it you can log events forever without discovering whether your account of yourself has become more accurate. The amendment line matters more. A record you never consult or use to change the next attempt is an archive of frustration.

Keep the original entry. If you later learn that you misremembered the time or omitted a constraint, append a correction. An unchangeable record of what you originally wrote and a visible route to amendment are both necessary. The first prevents convenient rewriting; the second prevents an early mistake from becoming permanent authority.

Some of the working notes call completed intentions divided by declared intentions a **self-trust score**. As a prompt, the fraction can reveal that you routinely promise six hours and deliver twenty minutes. As a target, it invites gaming: promise only trivial acts and you can score 100 percent while avoiding the real work. Ask instead whether the record is becoming more accurate, whether the chosen action still serves the value, and whether a justified correction changes tomorrow's plan. Self-trust grows through usable evidence, not a perfect denominator.

Review the entries once a week. Look for the cue that repeatedly precedes avoidance, the hour at which work reliably starts, and the kind of task that fails even when the phone is gone. Make **one** change to the next week's conditions. Do not redesign your entire life from one good or bad day.

## When the same loop returns

If a worry repeats, note what set it off and what you did after it appeared. Attention to a thought is not always rehearsal of it; sometimes noticing is how you stop acting on it. If the loop is fed by a feed, a person, or an argument that never changes when corrected, reduce exposure when you can. If it concerns trauma or persistent distress, a notebook and a two-minute rule may be useful aids, but they are not a substitute for skilled care.

If the record shows that you can start but still cannot make meaningful progress, check the position you occupy. Do you have the authority, time, tools, safety, and help the task requires? The finance chapters gave us this lesson at institutional scale: a valid claim and an effective remedy are different things. Here too, a personal practice can improve the moves available inside a position without changing the position itself. Sometimes the practical step is to ask for resources, change roles, negotiate a deadline, join with others, or leave a harmful arrangement. Do not make a private habit carry a public structure's debt.

## A seven-day trial

For one week, choose **one** valued project. Each morning, write one cue-linked action that takes two minutes to begin and has a visible finish. Before the cue, remove one avoidable friction. At the cue, notice the urge without treating it as a command. In the evening, write your prediction, plan, event, and next amendment. At the end of the week, reread all seven entries and ask:

1. Which prediction about myself was accurate, and which was too broad?
2. Which change in the room or schedule actually altered what I did?
3. Did the small beginning lead toward the larger work?
4. Which obstacle was a habit, and which was a constraint I cannot solve alone?
5. What single amendment will I carry into next week?

If the experiment yields five missed days, it has still produced evidence: perhaps the cue was unreliable, the task undefined, the hour impossible, or the environment hostile. Revise the design. The purpose is neither a streak nor a verdict. It is a better next decision.

The book began with a pattern that survives because it is maintained. You have watched records preserve skills, assign claims, alter readers, and guide machines. The practice here is smaller in scale and exactly the same in its demand: keep a trace of what mattered, let it be challenged by reality, and make the correction reach the next act. **You are the maintenance of the loop.** The freedom available to you begins wherever that maintenance can be made more truthful and more deliberate. The future outlook asks whether we will give the larger loops the same power to be corrected before they acquire still greater power over us.
