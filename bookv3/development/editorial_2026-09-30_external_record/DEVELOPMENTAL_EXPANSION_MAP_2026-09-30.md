# The Ledger Principle — Developmental expansion map

**Baseline at Pass 10:** 45,056 main-text words. Pass 11 added 249 source-backed words in Chapter 1, making the current total 45,305. The original 80,000–95,000-word estimate leaves 34,695–49,695 words to earn. Chapter length should follow the causal work a scene does. Do not expand a chapter merely to reach a number.

## First priority: let the gift cost something (Prologue and Chapters 1–2)

The first act is 5,569 words. Its bee, baker, Tasmanian, crow, and paper-plane examples establish transmission but pass quickly from an individual failed instruction to a civilization's map. Develop one concrete chain in which a mark is copied, misunderstood, corrected, and passed on again. Give the reader the failed attempt and its material cost before Henrich's demographic model. Then stage the strongest rival: an animal can learn socially, and a large population can still lose a skill. The resolution is access to a usable, correctable external record, not a claim that animal culture is zero or that headcount guarantees invention. Primary research already mapped in the Chapter 1–2 source notes should drive the scene. **Estimated useful addition: 3,000–5,000 words**, subject to an actual source walk.

## Second priority: follow one claimant or decision all the way through (Chapters 3–7)

The financial act's five chapters are 11,599 words. Develop the return clock in Chapter 3 and the recourse route in Chapter 4 with dated steps, the documents each actor could see, and the moment a rival explanation would predict a different outcome. In Chapter 5, stay longer with Green Mountain's filed claim and actual check before moving to class-level recovery data. In Chapters 6–7, let the reader follow the public account and the treaty award through distinct gates, including the point at which paper recognition becomes cash or does not. Preserve each case's instrument, denominator, jurisdiction, and outcome; the cases must never be made to look like one continuous transaction. The court opinions, filings, final accounts, and award already cited are the starting records. **Estimated useful addition: 5,000–8,000 words.**

## Third priority: make inheritance visible inside a reader and an agent (Chapters 8–13)

Act III is 16,947 words and carries the book's hardest conceptual change. Chapter 8 can stay with two different readers of one fable long enough to show what each actually supplies, then where interpretation needs an external check. Chapter 9 can make the S126 wrong-ledger failure legible in one complete attempt, with the positive memory-transfer control beside it. Chapter 10 needs a more patient passage from a teachable code to a lost distinction. Chapter 11 should let a reader inspect the original, the synthetic replacement, and the successful accumulate regime before declaring what deteriorated. Chapter 13's five-question card can return to one earlier financial and one machine example, showing how the same questions yield different remedies. Expand Chapter 12 only to clarify its rival tests; do not turn the consciousness hinge into a second book. Use the existing source walks and archived runs, with new primary checks wherever the prose adds a fact. **Estimated useful addition: 5,000–8,000 words.**

## Later decisions

The coda's 6,712 words already contains an executable seven-day practice; the 4,229-word outlook has ten falsifiable bets. Add to either only when a user of the practice or a reader of a forecast encounters a concrete missing step. After the three priority expansions, read the book as a single narrative and decide whether more length still improves it. The original estimate is a planning range, not evidence of quality. A shorter, complete book is preferable to a longer one that repeats the thesis.

For every addition, record **scene → failed expectation → strongest rival → decisive observation → metaphor boundary → person who bears the cost**.
