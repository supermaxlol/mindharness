# Chapter 7: The Border That Moved

On November 28, 2008, Sri Lanka's Supreme Court ordered its state oil company to stop paying several banks under oil-hedging contracts. Two citizens had brought fundamental-rights petitions. One challenged whether the Ceylon Petroleum Corporation had authority to make the deals at all. The order also called for an investigation and asked the Treasury to examine the price people were paying for petrol.

For a moment, the bill seemed to have arrived in the country's own public forum. The oil company had signed the contracts. Sri Lankans would live with the fuel prices and the corporation's losses. Citizens had gone to their Supreme Court and obtained an order that interrupted payment. Then a second legal map appeared. One of the banks was German. It could describe the same contract, and the state actions that stopped its payment, as an *investment* protected by a treaty between Germany and Sri Lanka. That description gave it a route to an international tribunal.

Four years later, the tribunal ordered Sri Lanka to pay Deutsche Bank $60,368,993, plus interest, and nearly $8 million in the bank's legal costs. The Supreme Court's interim orders had already been vacated. The treaty award remained.

The border in this story did not physically move. The contract's **legal address** did. Once the bank's rights under an oil hedge counted as a protected foreign investment, a dispute about a state-owned company's payments could also become a claim against the state in another forum. The people who lived beside the petrol stations had a domestic court; the bank had that treaty route as well. Each could challenge a record. Only one could invoke this particular cross-border promise.

That is the last mutation of the financial ledger in this act. We have seen cash move ahead of costs, assets and liabilities placed in different companies, valid claims shrink before payment, and banking losses pass through time into public accounts. Now the same question crosses a national boundary: **who can move a claim into another legal system, and what changes when it arrives?**

### The ceiling and the floor

The hedge itself is easier to see if you begin with an ordinary price guarantee. Imagine a shop buying fuel for the coming year. It worries that the price will climb, so it pays someone to soften the increase. The protection has a ceiling: if prices rise enough, the shop receives money, but only up to a stated maximum. If prices fall, however, it may owe money to the other side. The contract decides how far each side can gain or lose before anyone knows which direction prices will take.

On July 8, 2008, Dubai crude stood at $137.52 a barrel. Deutsche Bank and the Ceylon Petroleum Corporation agreed on a twelve-month arrangement tied to a strike price of $112.50 and 100,000 barrels a month. If the reference oil price rose above the strike, the bank would pay the corporation the difference, subject to a $10-per-barrel limit and a $2.5 million total target at which the trade would end. If the price fell below the strike, the corporation would pay the difference to the bank. The contract recorded no matching $2.5 million overall ceiling on those payments.

This was protection against one direction of movement with a much larger exposure in the other. That is a feature of the signed terms, not evidence by itself that a crime occurred. When oil was above $137, the fear of another rise was real. The bank took an obligation to pay if that fear materialized, and the corporation received that protection without a separate upfront premium recorded in the award. A hedge can be a sensible answer to volatile imports. The harder question is why this particular answer put a tight limit on the corporation's upside while leaving the cost of a deep price fall open.

The price fell. The tribunal's account records a small payment from Deutsche Bank to the corporation in September, then payments from the corporation to the bank of about $1.66 million in October and $4.51 million in November. By late November, the contracts were political as well as financial facts. The citizens' petitions challenged authorization and fairness. The Supreme Court suspended payments and directed further action. The central bank investigated and later issued a stop-payment direction. Deutsche Bank terminated its hedge in December and calculated a close-out amount of $60,368,993.

Here the price guarantee metaphor reaches its limit. A fuel hedge is a derivative: its value and the payment due on termination depend on a formula, a benchmark, and contractual terms about closing the position. The $60 million was not a stack of barrels that had vanished from a warehouse. It was the bank's claimed value of its contractual position after the market moved and the trade ended. To test that claim, one has to read the signed formula and the tribunal's valuation, then ask whether the claim has a valid route to payment.

### A second door in the courthouse

A bilateral investment treaty is a promise between two states about how each will treat qualifying investors from the other. Some treaties let those investors bring claims directly before an international arbitration tribunal when state conduct allegedly violates the treaty. The Germany–Sri Lanka treaty supplied Deutsche Bank with such a route. The relevant question was not only whether the oil company owed money under English-law contract documents. It was also whether Sri Lanka's courts and central bank had treated a protected investment in a way that breached the state's international promise.

Sri Lanka argued that this oil hedge was a commercial transaction, not the kind of investment the treaty and the ICSID Convention were meant to protect. It also defended the authorities' response to a controversial set of deals. A state must be able to investigate possible abuse, examine what its public companies signed, and protect its people from unlawful obligations. If every such investigation automatically became an international wrong, the state would be unable to govern its own agencies.

The tribunal's majority rejected the narrow reading of *investment*. It treated the bank's commitment and contractual rights as sufficient to cross the jurisdictional threshold. Then it examined what the authorities had actually done. Its award did not say that a citizen may never question a hedge or that a regulator may never investigate one. It found that the Supreme Court's far-reaching interim order had been made without properly hearing the banks and that the central bank's later process had serious defects, including failures of notice and an opportunity to respond. The majority concluded that Sri Lanka had breached the treaty's fair-and-equitable-treatment requirement and had deprived Deutsche Bank of the economic value of its rights.

That is the strongest answer to the charge that the treaty merely overrode democracy. The ruling turned on due process and specific state conduct, not just on the bank's wish to be paid. Protecting an investor from an arbitrary stop-payment order can protect the integrity of agreements on which a country itself depends. The procedural finding belongs in the story even if one thinks the hedge was a poor bargain.

But the boundary question survives that answer. A domestic contract alone would have led through its contract forum and against its named counterparty. Treaty jurisdiction created a second claim against the sovereign for the acts of its public institutions. The translation was not automatic: it depended on the claimant's nationality, the definition of investment, the tribunal's jurisdiction, and a proven treaty breach. In this case, those gates opened. The difference between a contractual claim against the corporation and an international award against Sri Lanka was not a change in oil. It was a change in which legal record could make the state answer.

One arbitrator disagreed at the first gate. Makhdoom Ali Khan read the treaty's definition to require a claim to money used to create economic value and associated with an investment. In his view, a debt arising from this hedge did not qualify. The disagreement was over the words that decided whether the second door existed at all. The majority's reading became the award; the dissent remains a map of the boundary it crossed.

### An award still needs a payer

The tribunal ordered $60,368,993 in compensation, interest running until payment, and $7,995,127.36 in legal fees and expenses. Sri Lanka sought annulment. The application was eventually discontinued, and the award was not set aside. Those facts establish a surviving international obligation. They do not, by themselves, tell us when, how much, or from which account Deutsche Bank was actually paid. The public case record we examined does not close that cash column.

This distinction matters because an international award is no magic withdrawal from a national treasury. Under the ICSID Convention, member states must recognize and enforce a monetary award as they would a final domestic judgment. Actual seizure of state property follows the law of the place where execution is sought, including rules on sovereign immunity. A tribunal can decide that a state owes money. It cannot reach into every public account in the world and take it.

So the treaty route has booths of its own: qualifying investor, protected investment, jurisdiction, breach, valuation, award, recognition, and execution. The route may be powerful even before the last booth. A credible prospect of an award changes the price of a dispute and the bargaining position of the parties. But a book about paid claims has to preserve the difference between *ordered* and *received*. Chapter 5 demanded that distinction of a bankrupt cooperative. A foreign bank is owed the same exacting accounting.

The Argentine bond case lets us see a treaty claim reach the final cash line. After Argentina's 2001 default, a large group of Italian holders of its sovereign bonds brought a claim under the Argentina–Italy investment treaty. Their case, *Abaclat and others v. Argentina*, involved more than 60,000 claimants. In 2011 a majority of the tribunal found it could hear the mass claim. A dissenter objected that the tribunal had stretched the treaty's consent and the meaning of investment too far. The underlying question was familiar: does owning this financial promise make its foreign holder an investor with a treaty right, or only a creditor with a bond claim?

No merits ruling decided that Argentina had violated the treaty. The parties settled. UNCTAD reports a settlement of $1.35 billion. More important than the rounded headline is the tribunal's own closing document: it says the claimants **had received payment** under the settlement. It also records the parties' insistence that the agreement admitted neither ICSID jurisdiction nor international liability or wrongdoing. The jurisdictional decision helped carry the dispute into a place where settlement was possible; the final record supplies cash, not a retrospective confession by Argentina.

Read the two cases together. In Sri Lanka, a derivative became a protected investment by majority ruling, a state was ordered to pay, and the available case record stops short of a verified payment. In Argentina, bonds entered the treaty forum, the parties avoided a merits judgment, and the settlement record says the holders were paid. Neither route makes every financial contract an investment. Both show the practical value of a second legal address for a claim.

### The claim never filed

The next objection is that treaties affect only disputes that reach a tribunal. A government can still vote, regulate, and defend itself. That is true. The question is whether the possibility of a claim changes the government's decision *before* a case exists.

Researchers call this **regulatory chill**. A regulator considering a new rule may narrow it, delay it, or abandon it because defending the rule would be costly or because an adverse award would be expensive. The mechanism is plausible: someone deciding whether to take a risk includes the expected cost of the fight. Yet plausibility is not proof that every delayed public measure was chilled by an investor treaty. To know that, we need evidence of what officials considered and what they changed.

A study of nutrition and alcohol policy in South Africa supplies exactly the kind of check the claim needs. Researchers interviewed officials and other participants about proposed regulations. They found that trade obligations mattered more to those decisions than investment-treaty fears. They reported very limited investor-state anticipatory chill in the nutrition and alcohol cases and no identified precedential chill there. They did find a reported delay of roughly two years in South Africa's tobacco plain-packaging proposal while officials watched disputes over Australia's similar policy. When Australia prevailed, that result increased officials' confidence to proceed.

The result is more useful than a slogan in either direction. A threatened cross-border dispute can alter the timing of a public decision. A state can also win, and its victory can make another state bolder. The pressure depends on the treaty, the policy, the expected cost of defending it, and the confidence of the officials making the decision. The shadow of a claim is a real part of the decision environment where the evidence shows it; it is not an all-purpose explanation for every policy failure.

Nor are governments locked forever inside the treaties they inherited. They can negotiate new terms, replace agreements, or terminate them under applicable rules. The difficulty is that some treaties keep protection alive for existing investments years after termination. UNCTAD's treaty-reform work records both the available choices and the long survival periods that can make a reform's effects slow to arrive. A vote can change the rules. It cannot make an existing promise disappear on the same afternoon simply by calling it obsolete.

### The isthmus

There is an old image of two seas meeting with a boundary between them. The Arabic word *barzakh* names that intervening boundary. In this chapter it is an image, not a legal theory: two bodies of water touch, but what passes between them depends on the seam.

The contract and the treaty met at such a seam. The same hedge was an obligation of a state oil company in one record and a protected investment in another. The Supreme Court could stop payments for a time; the tribunal could turn the state's handling of that stoppage into compensation. The citizens' petition crossed one set of gates; the bank's claim crossed another. Neither set of gates was imaginary. Each had consequences for the other.

The image breaks where the actual law begins. Water cannot draft a treaty, challenge jurisdiction, file an appeal, or change a rule. People can. The boundary was written by governments, interpreted by arbitrators, contested by citizens and investors, and capable of reform. Its force came from institutions acting on a shared record, not from a natural law of seas or an offshore spell.

That is why **“the state is powerless”** is the wrong ending. Sri Lankan citizens reached their Supreme Court. The tribunal itself had a dissent. Uruguay and Australia defended public-health measures against investor claims. Argentina negotiated and paid a settlement without admitting wrongdoing. States can govern, litigate, win, lose, settle, and rewrite future treaties. Their choices carry different costs because the legal paths available to different actors are unequal.

The harder ending is that sovereignty itself is written in overlapping ledgers. A government signs a contract, another government signs a treaty, a domestic court issues an order, an international tribunal reads the treaty, and a court elsewhere may be asked to recognize the award. To know who pays, follow the claim through *all* those records. The flag over the first courthouse does not settle the last one.

The ledger has learned to shift a payment in time, place a liability inside one company, rank a creditor behind another, transfer a failing bank's cost, and carry a claim across a border. None of these moves required the ledger to think. Every entry still needed a reader who would treat it as a reason to act. If the same words can lead one mind to question a story and another to obey it, where does the ledger's power reside: in the marks, or in the mind that completes them?
