# Chapter 4: The Company That Could Disappear

The note had a name on it. That was the problem.

When Big Lots sold KB Toys in 2000, it received cash and a $45 million promise to be paid later. The promise was a *pay-in-kind* note: interest could accumulate rather than arrive as cash each year. The company that issued it was Havens Corners Corporation, or HCC. On a chart of the toy business, HCC looked comfortably connected. Above it sat KB Holdings. Below it sat another holding company, then the operating subsidiaries with the stores, inventory, and customers.

The chart made all of these boxes look like one enterprise. The note named one box. HCC's only asset, the Delaware court later explained, was stock in the holding company below it. If the operating subsidiaries could not pay their own creditors, value would not climb the chain to HCC merely because all the boxes carried the same toy-store identity. Big Lots had accepted a claim that was subordinate to creditors nearer the stores.

The next chapter will follow a claim through every gate on its way to payment. Here is the gate that came first: *which company owed the money?*

In 2002, the operating subsidiaries borrowed and sent part of the proceeds upward. About $120 million was distributed to the buyout sponsor, affiliates, managers, and others, according to the court's account. Chapter 3 followed the dates of that payout. Now follow its address. The operating businesses took on the borrowing. The cash passed through HCC and up to KB Holdings, where shares were bought from owners. Big Lots' note stayed an HCC obligation.

When KB Toys entered Chapter 11 in January 2004, the note had grown to about $57 million with accrued interest. Big Lots sued the Bain affiliates and individuals, alleging that the refinancing had drained value from the business and left its note unpaid. It asked a Delaware court to treat the injury as one for which Big Lots could pursue those defendants directly.

The court dismissed the suit. Several of the asserted wrongs, it held, were injuries to the company as a whole. Claims for that injury belonged to the bankruptcy estate, which acts for creditors collectively, rather than to one creditor trying to recover first. Other pleaded claims failed on their own terms. The court did not decide in this opinion that the 2002 transaction caused the bankruptcy, and it did not calculate Big Lots' ultimate bankruptcy recovery.

That ruling served a real purpose. If the largest, best-lawyered unsecured creditor could take a company-wide claim out of bankruptcy and collect for itself, every smaller unsecured creditor would be left behind. The rule protected the collective process. It also closed Big Lots' attempted route to the people who had received the earlier cash. Both statements can be true.

### The name on the key

Imagine a corridor of locked rooms in one building. The sign outside says **KB Toys**. Your key says **HCC**. It opens the HCC room; it does not open every door bearing the same sign. In the HCC room you find shares in a room farther down the corridor. Before you can turn those shares into money, people with claims directly against that lower room must be paid.

The physical picture helps only up to a point. Corporate law is not a set of unbreakable doors. A guarantee can give a creditor a key to a parent. A lien can attach to a named asset. Insurance can fund a claim. A court can set aside a fraudulent transfer, or in some circumstances disregard a company's separate personality. Bankruptcy law may gather claims and property that individual lawsuits cannot. The contract, statute, and judgment decide which passages are open. A logo does not.

This is why the familiar statement “the company has plenty of money” may tell a claimant almost nothing. *Which company?* Does that company owe this debt? Did it guarantee another's debt? What asset backs the promise? What happens to the promise if the operating company fails? A consolidated balance sheet can add the rooms together for investors. A court still needs the legal route from claimant to asset.

Big Lots was no powerless bystander to its original bargain. The court emphasized that it was a sophisticated seller and had no contractual right to block the 2002 refinancing. It had taken a deeply subordinated note. The sensible rival explanation is therefore strong: perhaps this was simply a risk Big Lots priced and accepted. That defense explains the original note's priority. It does not make the later movement of cash, or the choice of defendant, disappear. The two facts together show the Ledger's second financial power: it can place an obligation at one address while value moves among related addresses.

The idea is easy to exaggerate. One might now draw a tiny liability company, put every debt inside it, put every asset elsewhere, and announce that creditors can recover nothing. That sketch misses the contracts crossing the corridor. The next real case nearly destroys it.

### A company built to hold the claims

Johnson & Johnson's consumer-products business had faced lawsuits over talc for years. The company disputes that its talc products caused the injuries alleged. The question in this chapter is separate from that dispute: how were the pending and future claims to be handled, and which balance sheet could be required to pay them?

In October 2021, J&J reorganized its consumer business through a divisional merger under Texas law. The old company ended. One successor, New Consumer, received the operating business and most of its assets. Another, LTL Management, received essentially all of the old company's talc-related liabilities. LTL then sought Chapter 11 protection. People who had brought talc claims would now face a company created to carry those claims into bankruptcy.

At first glance, the corridor picture seems to settle everything. Put the valuable brands in one room and the claimants in the other. Shut the door. But read the rest of the plan. LTL received cash, an interest in a royalty business, and, most importantly, a funding agreement. Under that agreement, J&J and New Consumer were jointly responsible for paying LTL's qualifying talc costs up to the value of New Consumer. The court described the right as worth roughly $61.5 billion at the time of filing, with a floor tied to New Consumer's value at the split. It was not a slip of paper against a penniless stranger.

The Third Circuit used that right to turn the company's argument back on itself. LTL had asked bankruptcy to protect it from the cost and disorder of mass litigation. The court asked whether the debtor actually faced the kind of financial distress that justified Chapter 11. A valuable, reliable payment right against J&J and New Consumer made the answer no on the record before it. In 2023 the court ordered the first LTL bankruptcy dismissed.

This is the failed walk the simple shell theory needs. The company that held liabilities was separate, but it was not sealed off from the assets behind the funding promise. The very bridge designed to support a bankruptcy resolution showed that LTL was not financially distressed enough to enter that forum. The split remained a way to assign claims to a particular entity; the courts and contracts determined whether that assignment could control the rest of the case.

What did the reorganization seek if it did not make parent money wholly unreachable? It sought a different route. A mass-tort bankruptcy can gather current and future claims, pause individual suits, value disputed claims, and, if a lawful plan is confirmed, channel payment through a trust. Those powers can help pay many people fairly when separate lawsuits threaten to consume the common pool. They can also replace a claimant's path to an operating company or jury with a slower, collective procedure. Moving the forum is a distributional act even when a funding agreement leaves substantial money on the table.

The next case shows that a similar corridor can remain closed to individual suits while that collective procedure continues.

### The door that stayed shut

Georgia-Pacific had faced asbestos claims arising from products sold decades earlier. In 2017 it used a Texas divisional merger to create New GP and Bestwall. New GP received most of the operating company's assets. Bestwall received the asbestos liabilities, about $32 million in cash, litigation-related contracts and insurance, a plaster business valued at roughly $145 million at filing, and a funding agreement under which New GP would cover specified expenses and, if necessary, a bankruptcy trust. Bestwall filed Chapter 11 that November.

Some claimants continued to name New GP in asbestos suits. Bestwall asked the bankruptcy court to halt those suits while it handled claims in one proceeding. The court granted a preliminary injunction. In 2023, the Fourth Circuit affirmed the injunction against suits involving the nondebtor affiliate. Its majority said the parallel claims against New GP could affect Bestwall's bankruptcy and that the funding agreement could make New GP's assets available through the estate for approved claims.

The dissent saw the direction of control differently. It argued that the companies had constructed the very relationships used to bring claims against New GP under the bankruptcy court's reach. The majority and dissent were reading the same agreements. They disagreed about what legal consequence those agreements should have. For claimants, the immediate consequence of the majority's ruling was concrete: the path of suing New GP separately remained blocked by the injunction while the bankruptcy process went on.

The later record matters. In August 2025 the Fourth Circuit also affirmed the denial of a separate attempt to dismiss Bestwall's bankruptcy for lack of subject-matter jurisdiction. That did not award any claimant a particular sum or confirm a final trust. It did mean that, on the questions those appeals presented, this corporate and procedural arrangement continued to operate. The earlier draft of this book said Bestwall had been dismissed along with the J&J cases. It had not. That error erased the strongest example of the boundary doing what its designers sought: directing claims away from individual suits against the operating successor while a funding promise supplied a route through bankruptcy.

One court of appeals dismissed LTL's first bankruptcy because the funding right made the debtor too financially secure for Chapter 11 under the rule it applied. Another sustained an injunction in Bestwall and later rejected a jurisdictional dismissal challenge. The companies, contracts, claims, legal tests, and jurisdictions differed. The conclusion is sharper than “the trick always works” or “the trick is dead.” The person who drafts the boundary can change a claimant's route; a court's reading of the funding bridge determines how far that change survives.

### The vote that did not become a voice

J&J tried again. Its 2024 Red River Talc plan proposed roughly $9 billion to resolve ovarian and other gynecological cancer claims. It sought votes before the bankruptcy filing, aiming to arrive with the support needed for a prepackaged settlement. The new liability company and its parent presented a plan that, if confirmed, would route claims through a trust and release some claims against others.

There was a strong case for wanting one settlement. Tens of thousands of people had claims. J&J maintained that the claims lacked merit; claimants and their lawyers disputed that. A single process could avoid endless, unequal trials and give future claimants a share. Many claimants and firms supported the plan. A reader need not assume everyone who voted yes had been fooled to see what the court had to decide.

The bankruptcy judge examined the record of the votes. He found serious solicitation and tabulation problems, including too little time for thousands of claimants to make a choice and difficulties establishing who had authority to vote for whom. The court could not certify that the required class had accepted the plan. It also found other barriers to confirmation, including improper nonconsensual releases of claims against parties outside bankruptcy and an overbroad attempt to channel claims against retailers. On March 31, 2025, it denied confirmation and dismissed Red River's case for cause.

The judge's short sentence belongs at the center of this book: “Votes are voices.” A mark in a voting ledger can authorize a settlement that binds people. If a lawyer's mark does not reliably represent a client's decision, the page may be full while the court has not heard the people whose claims would be changed. The judge did not decide the underlying science of talc in that order. He decided that this record could not authorize this plan.

J&J said it would return to the tort system rather than appeal. It also announced that it would reverse approximately $7 billion it had reserved for the proposed bankruptcy resolution. That accounting change was neither cash paid to claimants nor a judgment that the injuries had vanished. It was the company's next entry after the chosen route failed. The pending claims still needed a forum and a result.

Here the Ledger's power becomes visible at human scale. A divisional merger chooses the name on the defendant's door. A funding agreement chooses what can pass between rooms. An injunction chooses where a claimant may speak. A ballot chooses whose consent can make a new plan binding. None of those is merely a mirror held up to a preexisting cost. Each is a record that changes the next move.

### Follow the passage, not the logo

There is another boundary in financial reports, and confusing it with the legal one makes the story sound simpler than it is. In Chapter 3, an accounting rule brought Bank of America's existing securitization vehicles into its main balance sheet. Other bank filings disclose a *maximum exposure to loss* from vehicles they do not consolidate. That number estimates what the **bank** could lose from its interests and commitments to a vehicle. It is not, by itself, the amount a creditor of that vehicle can legally collect from the bank. Accounting consolidation changes reported assets, liabilities, and ratios. A guarantee, lien, contract, statute, or judgment determines legal recourse. One page may inform the other; they are not the same page.

So the appealing formula “empty company, zero recovery” survives only where the company truly has no reachable assets *and* no enforceable bridge to a solvent party, insurance, or other source of payment. LTL's funding agreement was a bridge. Bestwall's agreement was a bridge, while its injunction controlled the route by which claimants could use it. Big Lots had a note at a particular level of a toy-store chain and no contractual veto over the later payout. These are different architectures, and each produced a different next decision.

The hardest defense of corporate boundaries is also the best one: they let people invest, lend, insure, and reorganize without every failure consuming every related enterprise. A bankruptcy trust may protect future victims from a race in which the first plaintiffs take everything. Big Lots' dismissed direct suit preserved equal treatment among unsecured creditors. A boundary can make a common solution possible. That is precisely why control of the boundary is valuable. The same rule that prevents one creditor from jumping the queue can decide how long every creditor waits and which assets enter the queue at all.

The Ledger has now learned to choose an address. But naming the company and reaching a court still do not tell us whether a person will be paid. Big Lots held a real note. Talc claimants held or asserted real causes of action. In every case, a claim had to be recognized, defended, ranked, valued, and finally collected. The next chapter follows one of those claims through the tollbooths between a right and a payment.
