# Chapter 5: The Tollbooths

On June 7, 2024, a bankruptcy trustee issued a payment to Green Mountain Feeds. The claim number was 2. The amount Green Mountain had asked to be paid was $1,078.35. The amount the trustee's final account recognized was also $1,078.35. No reduction appeared in the *allowed* column.

The payment was $333.39.

There is a story inside those three entries: **asserted, allowed, paid**. A person can win the middle one and still lose most of the money. In this case, $744.96 of an allowed claim never reached the supplier through the bankruptcy distribution. No judge needed to declare the bill imaginary. The estate could recognize it precisely and pay only 30.92 percent.

The debtor was Central CT Coop, a Connecticut agricultural cooperative. It filed for Chapter 7 liquidation in August 2016. Green Mountain's payment went out nearly eight years later. The trustee's final account, filed that December, reported that the case had been fully administered. It lists the supplier's claim in an exhibit and records the actual payment again in a cash-disbursement register, down to the check number: 30009. This is as close as an ordinary reader can get to seeing an official right turn into cash on the same page.

The gap between those columns is the subject of this chapter. We have already watched an owner receive cash before a company paid its debts, then watched legal boundaries determine which company a claimant could sue. Now the claimant has reached the correct room. A claim is on the books. Why is the money still missing?

### An address is not a payment

Imagine you are owed a refund by a store that has closed. You still have the receipt. The store manager agrees that the amount is correct. Then she opens the till and shows you that several people have claims on the same cash, some with rights to be paid before you. The receipt has done its job: it proves what is owed. It cannot fill the till.

Bankruptcy law formalizes that moment. A creditor files a proof of claim. Another party can object. The court may allow or disallow the claim, or allow only part of it. Then the law and the terms of any collateral determine the order in which money is distributed. A creditor can be right about the amount and still be late in the order, or face a pool too small for everyone whose claims have been allowed.

The law is not simply a conspiracy against the last person in line. If everyone could collect the whole of an insolvent debtor's remaining money as soon as they found it, the quickest or best-funded creditors would empty the account. A collective proceeding can preserve value, test claims, and divide what remains according to stated rules. The strongest defense of the tollbooths is therefore fairness: without proof, priority, and a common distribution, there may be no meaningful equality at all.

Green Mountain's check does not refute that defense. It exposes the price hidden inside the word *fair*. In this case, the final account reports $1,051,802.81 paid on allowed secured claims, $59,780.44 paid on a priority unsecured claim, $496,887.88 in Chapter 7 administration expenses, and $50,142.69 paid on allowed general unsecured claims. Those entries add to the estate's $1,658,613.82 of net receipts and disbursements. They did not all draw on an identical, freely interchangeable pile: secured claims can be tied to collateral and its proceeds. But they show the different results of passing through different legal categories in one closed case.

The priority unsecured claimant was a health services and insurance plan for Teamsters Local 559. Its $59,780.44 allowed claim was paid in full. Green Mountain's $1,078.35 allowed claim was paid at 30.92 percent. The difference was not that one arithmetic problem was solved correctly and the other incorrectly. The difference was the legal character of the claims and the money available at each point in the distribution. A classification became a payment rule. The page decided who absorbed the shortage.

For Green Mountain, the final report does not tell us whether this particular bill threatened a worker's wages, a delivery, or the company's survival. It would be dishonest to invent those consequences. What it does tell us is enough: the recognized amount and the paid amount differ by $744.96. The unpaid amount stayed with the creditor when the estate closed.

### What each booth actually does

The word *claim* makes the process sound like one question: do you have a right to be paid? In practice it is a sequence of different questions, answered by different people and records.

First is the **entry booth**. Did the creditor learn of the case, identify the debtor, and file the required proof in the required way and time? The court's final account lists many scheduled creditors marked “N/F”: no proof of claim filed. A name on a debtor's old list is not the same entry as a filed claim eligible for distribution. We cannot tell from that mark alone why any particular creditor did not file. The important fact is that the record distinguishes the two.

Second is the **recognition booth**. Was the filed amount allowed? Under federal bankruptcy law, a filed proof is generally deemed allowed unless a party in interest objects; an objection can bring the amount and legal basis before the court. Central CT Coop's final account separates the dollars creditors *asserted* from the dollars *allowed*. For general unsecured claims, it reports $1,490,398.95 asserted and $162,184.18 allowed. The report does not explain every change in those rows. Some claims may have been amended, duplicated, compromised, disallowed, or reclassified; a total alone cannot say which. It does show why “creditors asked for $1.49 million” cannot be silently restated as “the court found $1.49 million payable.”

Green Mountain passed that booth intact. The whole $1,078.35 was allowed.

Third is the **rank booth**. What kind of claim has been allowed? Secured creditors have rights against specified collateral. Certain unsecured claims receive statutory priority. General unsecured claims come behind higher-ranking claims and the costs of administering the estate. The hierarchy can be defended as the price of lending against collateral, protecting particular vulnerable claims, and funding a process that collects and distributes assets for everyone. Yet the hierarchy is also a record of bargaining power and political choice. A creditor who can demand collateral before lending may buy a better place in line. A small supplier usually sells on terms already written by someone else.

Fourth is the **collection booth**. After the claim is recognized and ranked, what value can actually be reached? An empty debtor does not become solvent because an order says “allowed.” A trust, guarantor, insurance policy, lien, or recovery action may enlarge the reachable pool. Without an enforceable bridge, an asset held by some related but separate company is not automatically available. In the last chapter, LTL's funding agreement showed why the phrase “liability company with no assets” could be false. Here the question is the same in a more ordinary setting: what money, from which legal source, can make the final entry payable?

Finally comes **execution**. A distribution has to be made, not merely proposed. The distinction can seem pedantic until a check is voided, replaced, or never clears. Central CT Coop's report records one creditor's initial check as voided in October 2024 and a later payment to the transferee of that claim. For Green Mountain, the report records the June distribution without such a reversal. The trustee certified that funds on hand had been distributed. That transaction line, and not the allowed-claim line by itself, is the cash evidence.

These are tollbooths because each can stop or shrink a route. But they are not five clerks taking turns to steal from the same bag. Some screen bad claims; some determine priority; some reveal an actual shortage that existed before the claim was filed. Their decisions can be challenged, and successful challenges can change the amount or the assets available. The point is that a right must survive the whole route to become a remedy.

### The multiplication a small claimant feels

Suppose 100 creditors each have the same $100 claim and face four independent hurdles, each of which lets 70 percent of the remaining claims through. The arithmetic is simple: 0.7 multiplied by itself four times is 0.2401. About 24 of the 100 claims reach the far side *in that imagined system*. If passing claims are then paid in full, the expected payment averaged across the original creditors is about $24 per $100 claimed. Add another hurdle and the share falls again. No single booth needs to look severe for the compound effect to be severe.

That is the teaching value of the image. It is not a measurement of bankruptcy claims. Real gates are rarely independent, their passage rates are not known from a tally of court search results, and claimants adapt. A missing document may cause both a filing problem and a proof problem; an amended claim may reopen a route; a successful objection to an objection may protect the creditor. Green Mountain did **not** receive 30.92 percent because it passed four 70-percent gates. Its allowed claim received the documented percentage available to its class in that estate.

The useful question is therefore not “Is the four-gate number true?” It is: **which decision or shortage took the money between one column and the next?** In the Connecticut report, recognition did not reduce Green Mountain's claim. The reduction happened between allowance and distribution. Improving only the ease of filing would not have supplied the missing $744.96. To change that result, one would have to change the assets recovered, the claims ahead of it, the legal ranking, or the applicable source of payment. A reform aimed at the wrong booth may produce more beautifully recorded rights and the same small checks.

Our research notes call the fraction admitted through recognition gates *psi*, the ceiling imposed by reachable resources *K*, and the share actually paid *phi*. A reader does not need the letters to understand the trap. A filed amount, an allowed amount, and a paid amount are three different numbers. Still, the shorthand is useful when a large system tries to fold them into one. Ask for the first number, the second, and the third. If someone provides only the first, you cannot infer recognition. If they provide only the second, you cannot infer payment. If they provide only the third, you cannot know how much was lost on the route.

The hardest version of this problem appears when the pool itself is capped. Imagine a genuine $100 claim, all procedures cleared, and only $10 in legally reachable assets. Making the claim easier to file cannot produce an eleventh dollar. A successful clawback, guarantee, insurance claim, or other lawful source could raise that ceiling; the cap is a legal and factual question, not a fixed property of nature. Until someone changes that source of payment, though, recognition and recovery move on separate tracks.

### A pattern bigger than one cooperative

Perhaps Central CT Coop is an odd small bankruptcy. A single liquidation cannot tell us how every company or every court treats creditors. So we looked at a different population: the Florida–UCLA–LoPucki Bankruptcy Research Database of large public-company bankruptcies. Its January 2023 release contains 1,218 cases. Our calculation uses the subset for which both secured and general unsecured class claims and distributions are recorded in the same case: 105 cases, most from Chapter 11 rather than the Connecticut cooperative's Chapter 7.

In that matched subset, the median secured class recovered the full recorded claim. The median general unsecured class recovered 20 cents per dollar. Secured recovery exceeded unsecured recovery in 95 of the 105 cases. The exact percentages should not be carried from those large cases into Green Mountain's small one. The common mechanism is what matters. Across radically different estates and procedures, the line labeled *allowed* is not the line labeled *paid*, and the legal rank of the claim predicts which gap a creditor is likely to face.

The earlier research draft of this book put the unsecured median at 21.3 cents while calling it a within-case comparison. That 21.3-cent figure comes from the larger set of 129 cases with unsecured recovery data; the *matched* 105-case calculation is 20 cents. The distinction is a little under two cents, but it changes what the sentence claims to compare. We corrected it because this book's own ledger should be answerable to the same three-column test it asks of everyone else.

A second kind of record points in the same direction. Moody's study of defaulted debt found lower trading-price recoveries as bond claims moved down the seniority ladder: a mean of 57.4 cents per dollar of face value for senior secured debt versus 28.9 cents for junior subordinated debt in its 1982–2004 recovery observations. Those are market prices around default, not the final checks from Central CT Coop, and the people holding those instruments may have bought them at different prices. The numbers do not establish that every secured creditor is treated justly or that every unsecured creditor is a victim. They establish that priority is valuable enough for markets to price it before final payment arrives.

The best objection is still alive: hierarchy and proof are necessary in a world with scarce assets. Yes. That is why the moral question cannot be answered by pointing at a low recovery alone. A secured lender may have supplied funding that kept the business open for years. A priority health plan may be protecting workers. A trustee's fees may be the cost of turning scattered property into any payment at all. The question this chapter asks is more exacting: **who had a chance to set the categories and prices before the shortage, and who encountered them only when the bill came due?** A system can distribute scarce money according to its rules and still make the weaker party carry a risk that was never visible in the price they received.

That is why “the claim was valid” is such an incomplete ending. Green Mountain's was valid in the precise sense recorded by the trustee: fully allowed. The next column recorded 30.92 percent. The estate's closing report made the remaining 69.08 percent legible as a loss borne there, by that creditor, in that case. Recognition did not erase the shortage. It named its bearer.

### The amount no one can make vanish

Once the final distribution is made, the ledger has to put the missing value somewhere. Sometimes it sits with a supplier as an unpaid receivable. Sometimes it sits with a pension, a lender, an insurer, or a government. A company can report one value for an asset while its eventual recovery will be another. Between those dates, the gap has not disappeared. It has merely lacked a final address.

Green Mountain's $744.96 has a final address in the report: the creditor. The next chapter follows the harder case, when losses remain in the system for years while the records continue to say that loans are performing and capital is sound. When that accumulated gap finally demands payment, who gets the check—and who gets the bill?
