# Chapter 6: The Cost That Would Not Stay Buried

On May 30, 2025, the British government sold its last shares in NatWest. The transaction ended a relationship that had begun seventeen years earlier, when the bank was still called the Royal Bank of Scotland and the government had stepped in to keep it alive.

The Treasury published the closing account. It had put £45.5 billion into RBS in 2008 and 2009. It eventually received £34.98 billion back through share sales, dividends, and fees. The difference was about £10.5 billion.

Seventeen years is a long time to wait for the last column of an invoice. The Treasury did not claim that letting RBS fail would have saved £10.5 billion. It said the opposite: a collapse would likely have cost the country far more in lost savings, damaged businesses, and broken confidence. That may well be right. The rescue can have been necessary and the public can still have paid for it. Those are separate entries.

The striking fact is when the bill became final. The government's cash went out while the crisis was still unfolding. The value it could recover depended on a bank, a market, and a decade and a half of later decisions. Only when the last share was sold could the Treasury close this particular account.

In the previous chapter, Green Mountain Feeds had an allowed claim of $1,078.35 and received $333.39. The missing $744.96 had a visible address: the creditor. A banking crisis creates a harder problem. The gap between what a loan is expected to repay and what it eventually repays can widen across thousands of borrowers before anyone knows its final size. Meanwhile, depositors can ask for their money today. When the gap finally has to be covered, the bill may move from shareholders to creditors, to the state, and sometimes to anyone holding money whose buying power falls. Each move leaves a different kind of record. To follow the cost, we have to resist treating those records as the same thing.

### Two clocks in the same bank

Imagine a landlord whose tenants promise rent over the next ten years while the landlord's own lender can demand repayment next month. The rental income may be real. The property may be valuable. But if the lender calls and the landlord cannot sell or borrow quickly enough, the landlord is in trouble *today*, long before the ten years of rent have been collected.

A bank lives with a more sophisticated version of that mismatch. Its assets include loans that pay over time and securities whose sale prices can change rapidly. Its liabilities include deposits and borrowing that may need to be paid or rolled over much sooner. One clock asks, “How much will these assets ultimately yield?” The other asks, “Can you pay this afternoon?” An institution can fail when the second clock strikes even though the first question has no settled answer.

RBS made both clocks more dangerous. Before the crisis, it had expanded its lending and structured-credit business. In 2007 it joined a consortium that bought ABN AMRO. The Financial Services Authority's later investigation found that RBS funded much of its part of the acquisition with debt, most of it short-term, while taking on more exposure to assets whose value was becoming uncertain. The acquisition increased its dependence on the very kind of borrowing that disappears when lenders lose confidence. The regulator's account is blunt about the due diligence supplied to RBS in April 2007: two lever-arch folders and a CD, with little further private information successfully obtained. A decision of enormous scale traveled through a remarkably thin reading room.

The market was already changing. Structured-credit markets deteriorated from the spring of 2007. RBS's supervisors later found that its credit exposures, leverage, and dependence on wholesale funding had made it especially vulnerable. Yet the losses that would eventually be recorded on ordinary loans could not all be known in the autumn of 2008. Across 2007–10, RBS recognized £32.5 billion of impairment on loans and advances. Losses on credit trading came to another £17.7 billion over the period examined by the regulator. These are different categories; neither figure is the government's £10.5 billion eventual shortfall.

That distinction is the chapter's first trap. One account describes damage to the bank's assets. Another records what the government spent to stabilize the bank. A third records what the government recovered. If we call all three “the loss,” we can make almost any conclusion sound certain. But a creditor who has not repaid a bank is not the same as a taxpayer whose government owns a share in that bank. Their accounts are connected; they are not interchangeable.

There was nothing mystical about how a bank could report an asset before knowing its eventual recovery. RBS's 2008 accounts said a customer loan carried at amortized cost was impaired when there was objective evidence that events since the loan was granted had changed expected cash flows. The bank assessed individual loans and portfolios and made provisions. It did not keep *every* bad loan at face value until a sudden secret day of confession. Nor could it know every future default the day a loan was made. The accounting rule turned on evidence available at a date; the borrower's ability to pay turned on a future that kept changing.

This is where a loss can be stored in the economic sense. A loan may be worth less than the number a bank will ultimately collect while the shortfall is still uncertain, incompletely reflected in provisions, or not yet realized in cash. Our research notes call the gap between the best current expectation of recovery and the value still carried in the records **stored loss**, or *S*. It is a way of asking where a prospective shortfall resides, not a line item that an auditor can lift directly from a balance sheet. As evidence improves, the bank recognizes provisions, writes down assets, sells them, or recovers more than feared. The size of *S* changes. The remaining loss must eventually be assigned somewhere.

The landlord analogy breaks here. A mortgage on one building has a small set of tenants and lenders; a major bank has interlocking claims across countries and markets. Its confidence problem can damage other banks and businesses before its assets have been resolved. That is why a delayed loss can become a public emergency rather than remaining a private accounting dispute.

### The bill comes through a side door

RBS's own funding arrangements supplied a preview. Some assets had been placed in commercial-paper conduits: vehicles financed by short-term notes and backed by longer-lived assets. The bank had promised liquidity if those vehicles could not roll their paper. At the end of June 2008, £8.6 billion had been drawn and remained outstanding under committed facilities to RBS's and ABN AMRO's own sponsored conduits. The FSA later called this a significant drain on RBS's liquidity.

This is a familiar household move at an unfamiliar scale. You guarantee someone else's rent because you believe they will pay it themselves. As long as they do, the promise looks quiet. When their landlord knocks on your door, the guarantee becomes a demand for cash. The conduit promise was not necessarily an unrecorded legal obligation, and £8.6 billion drawn was not £8.6 billion lost. Its lesson is about timing: a promise that once seemed remote can compete for cash precisely when cash is hardest to find.

By 2008 the bank had too many such questions arriving together: the price of risky assets, the quality of loans, the ability to refinance, and the willingness of others to trust the answers. The FSA's investigation would later name six factors in RBS's failure, including weak capital, short-term funding, asset uncertainty, credit-trading losses, the ABN AMRO deal, and the wider crisis. It also faulted regulation and supervision. There was no single concealed barrel of loss that, when opened, explained the entire collapse. There was a system built to operate while each part could rely on the next one.

That is the “failed walk” of a rescue. A regulator first tries to distinguish a temporary cash squeeze from a permanent shortage. A bank may be unable to borrow because lenders are frightened, or lenders may be frightened because the assets will not repay. Delay can deepen both problems. If the authorities wait for every loan's final outcome, the payment system can seize up before the answer arrives. If they supply support too cheaply or without conditions, private decision-makers may keep the gains and leave the downside to the public. By the time the question is urgent, the menu of painless choices is already gone.

The British government bought shares to provide capital. It also offered a second kind of protection whose terms show exactly how a ledger assigns risk. Under the 2009 Asset Protection Scheme, £282 billion of RBS assets were placed in a covered pool. RBS would bear the first £60 billion of losses on those assets. Beyond that threshold, the Treasury would bear 90 percent of additional losses and RBS 10 percent. RBS would pay fees for the cover.

Think of the excess on an insurance policy. The owner pays the first part of a claim; the insurer takes much of the tail. Here the insurer was the public. A line had been drawn across an uncertain future: losses below it belonged to the bank, most losses beyond it would belong to taxpayers. No cash needed to cross that line on the day the contract was signed for the assignment of risk to be real.

The part many versions of this story leave out is what happened next. RBS exited the scheme in October 2012 after paying £2.5 billion in fees. The Treasury reported **no payouts** under the Asset Protection Scheme. The state had carried contingent risk and collected a premium; it did not pay a £282 billion claim, or any other claim under that scheme. A covered asset pool is not an amount of public loss. A risk transfer is not proof that the risk materialized.

The government nevertheless did lose money on its *shareholding*, as the 2025 closing account shows. This is the exact address of the visible fiscal shortfall. Even here, the £10.5 billion difference is a nominal cash comparison over seventeen years, not a complete estimate of the rescue's social cost or benefit. It omits the counterfactual cost of collapse and does not turn itself into a measured rise in prices. Its force is simpler: the bill did not evaporate when the bank survived. Part of it remained with the public owner when the final shares were sold.

### What the rescue actually saved

The best defense of the intervention deserves its full strength. RBS served more than forty million customers and operated in over fifty countries. A disorderly failure could have trapped deposits, interrupted businesses, pulled down other institutions, and deepened the recession. The Treasury explicitly argues that the alternative would have cost more than the £10.5 billion cash shortfall. It is possible to agree and still ask why the earlier decisions created a choice between two costly endings.

The opposite slogan—“every bailout is a gift that taxpayers never recover”—also fails when tested against completed accounts. In the United States, the Government Accountability Office tracked the Troubled Asset Relief Program through its close in 2023. Across its programs, $443.5 billion was disbursed and the lifetime net cost after repayments, sales, dividends, interest, and other income was $31.1 billion. The Capital Purchase Program, which invested in banks, returned a *net gain* of $16.3 billion. Other programs had costs and different intended beneficiaries. Gross rescue money, net public cost, and the benefit of avoiding a collapse were three different columns there too.

So the conclusion cannot be that states always lose, or that states should always stand aside. It is that a rescue is a decision about **which claims remain whole while the shortage is worked through**. The answer is written into the price of state capital, the rank of old shareholders and creditors, the guarantees offered, the fees charged, the losses eventually realized, and the conditions imposed afterward. “We saved the bank” says something important about the system's survival. It says too little about the distribution of the bill.

The American banking records from the same crisis show why timing mattered beyond RBS. Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation data for insured U.S. banks report $176.2 billion in provisions for loan and lease losses for 2008 and $247.7 billion for 2009. Net charge-offs rose from $100.4 billion to $186.8 billion. Those are yearly flows for the U.S. banking sector, not a measurement of RBS and not a count of all hidden losses. They show the recognized cost catching up as borrowers stopped paying and banks revised expectations. An earlier draft of this book called a separately extracted $233.6 billion figure a single-quarter provision and treated a credit-to-GDP gap as if it measured the stock of unseen losses. The official FDIC series does not support that reading. Correcting the entry makes the mechanism clearer: accounting recognition has a pace, and a credit cycle can outrun it, but neither a provision nor a trend gap tells us the size of a secret vault.

### When the bill reaches prices

There is another route by which a banking shortage can reach the public, and it needs a different scene. If a government raises taxes or borrows to fund a rescue, the cost enters public finances. If a central bank creates and sustains additional money or credit support amid collapsing confidence, the result can also press on the currency and prices. The channels can overlap. They are not a machine that prints a fixed percentage of inflation for each unit of rescue money.

Picture a room with one hundred units of money offered for one hundred units of goods. If the goods available fall to eighty while the same money is still spent in the room, each unit of goods costs 100 divided by 80, or 1.25 times as much. That is a 25 percent price rise in the toy room. The holder of an old unit of money can now buy four-fifths of what it could before. This is the thought experiment behind the earlier draft's ratio *100/80*.

The room is deliberately small. Real economies make more goods, import them, save or spend at different speeds, change interest rates, and alter expectations. A central-bank loan can be repaid and withdrawn. A newly created reserve is not automatically a pound spent at the supermarket. The 100/80 ratio shows what happens to fixed nominal claims when real backing shrinks under stipulated conditions; it does **not** calculate inflation from the RBS rescue. To say that it did would hide the very transfer we are trying to inspect behind a false equation.

Indonesia's banking crisis in 1997–98 shows a monetary rescue channel in the world beyond the toy room. Bank Indonesia extended emergency liquidity to banks facing withdrawals. An IMF study found that outstanding support rose from about 3 trillion rupiah in August 1997 to more than 140 trillion by June 1998. Another IMF account describes how liquidity entering circulation, as depositors withdrew money, added to the money supply. Indonesia's own April 1998 letter to the IMF said the support put pressure on the exchange rate and reserves. In that same document, the authorities reported steep monthly consumer-price increases and identified the depreciating currency and severe drought as major contributors. Inflation was a crisis outcome with several causes, not a number that can be assigned solely to bank support.

What makes the episode revealing is the path through the records. Assistance first appeared as central-bank liquidity. Some of it ceased to look like a short-term bridge and became support for banks whose assets could not cover their obligations. The government then planned bonds to reimburse the central bank and account for restructuring costs. A banking claim had moved through monetary and fiscal ledgers, while households encountered the crisis in the prices of food and imports. The record changed form each time; the constraint that someone had to absorb the gap remained.

This is the point where the insurance metaphor also runs out. A state can tax, borrow, guarantee, create money through its central bank, restructure claims, or combine those tools. Each changes the timing and the bearer in a different way. Inflation can transfer buying power from holders of nominal money and claims, but it is never honest to point to a CPI chart and declare that every percentage point is a particular bank's unpaid bill. In Britain, the RBS record gives a measured fiscal shortfall. In Indonesia, the primary accounts document emergency liquidity alongside exchange-rate and price pressure, with other shocks plainly present. The two cases establish routes. They do not license a single universal invoice.

### Who gets to close the account?

At the end of the RBS story, the bank existed. Depositors had not been made to wait seventeen years for the government to sell its final share. Businesses had continued to pay workers and receive payments. These are real gains from keeping a financial system running. They do not change the fact that the Treasury closed its own RBS account about £10.5 billion short of the cash it had put in.

The more dangerous ledger trick is to stop reading before the final page. A loan may be called performing because its payments are current while the borrower is losing the capacity to finish the term. A guarantee may be called free because nobody has drawn it yet. A rescue may be called a £45.5 billion loss before any share is sold, or a complete success before its last share is priced. Each sentence freezes a moving process at the moment most useful to its speaker.

The disciplined question is not merely “What is this asset worth today?” It is: **what claim is being kept whole while the answer is deferred, what promise will be called if the first one fails, and who has the power to close the account?** RBS's final sale lets us answer for one public investment. Many losses never arrive at so neat a closing date. Some are carried through loans and guarantees; some become diminished payments to creditors; some are borne in taxes, spending, or purchasing power. The format changes, but a cost that cannot be collected from one party must find another bearer or remain an unpaid claim.

In the first five chapters, the ledger crossed a life, a generation, a company, and a court. Here it crossed time. The people who made the promises and the people who lived with their final price did not all meet at the same table. The next chapter follows the same problem across a border. If a state can be left with the bill, what happens when the owner of the gains can move the place where the bill may be collected?
