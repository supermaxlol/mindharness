# Chapter 3: Cash Before Cost

On April 23, 2002, the toys did not leave the shelves. The cash did.

KB Toys' operating companies borrowed. Some of the proceeds paid bonuses to more than fifty managers and executives. The rest traveled upward through a chain of holding companies. At the top, KB Holdings used the money to buy back 65 percent of its shares from Bain Capital affiliates and other insiders. A Delaware court later described the transaction in the plainest useful terms: it was a liquidity event that let the private equity investors withdraw money from the toy business.

The ownership chain matters, and we will follow it in the next chapter. For now, watch the calendar. The investors received cash in 2002. The operating businesses retained the new debt and the job of meeting its payments in the years ahead. The court record does not give a single tidy amount for every recipient. A later study, drawing on published reporting, says Bain had invested $18 million of equity in the original purchase and cites an $85 million payout from the recapitalization, about a 370 percent return on that equity by the study's calculation. The direction of travel is visible in the court's account: money moved from lenders into the business and then out to owners, while the obligation to repay stayed with the business.

Twenty-two months later, on January 14, 2004, KB Toys filed for Chapter 11. Big Lots, the former owner and a creditor through a note from the sale, alleged that the 2002 transaction had left the toy companies insolvent. The court did not establish that accusation as fact. In the case brought by Big Lots, it dismissed all counts. KB Toys also faced brutal retail competition and expensive mall leases. The interval from payout to bankruptcy is a fact; a straight line of causation between them is a proposition that has to be tested.

But the timing itself does not depend on winning that proposition. In 2002, the payout was complete. The company's future capacity to service its new borrowing had yet to be proved. If the business prospered, creditors could be paid and the investors' early cash might be an ordinary, successful return. If it weakened, the owners would still have received that distribution. The company, its lenders, workers, and later claimants would face the next decision with less room to absorb the shock.

That difference is the first financial move of the Ledger. An entry can turn tomorrow's expected receipts into today's payment to someone who will not necessarily be present when tomorrow arrives.

### Two clocks on one desk

Imagine a bakery with a reliable stream of customers. Its owner borrows against the income expected over the next several years and pays the borrowed money to herself. The ovens have not improved. No new shop has opened. Yet the owner has cash today, and the bakery has a larger loan schedule.

This is an analogy, not an allegation that every dividend recapitalization is reckless. A bakery owner who keeps ownership may bear the later cost herself. In a private fund, money paid out may pass from a company to its owner and on to the fund's investors; the company and the recipients have different balance sheets. The difference between those balance sheets is the point.

A dividend recapitalization, or dividend recap, turns a firm's borrowing capacity into a distribution without selling the firm. The company takes on debt. Its owners take a payout. From then on, the same operating cash that might have paid for wages, equipment, rent, or old creditors must also pay interest and principal on the new debt. A strong company may manage this comfortably. A weak one may not. In either case, the dates of receipt and obligation are separated.

The first clock is the closing date. The lender advances cash, the sponsor records a distribution, and the fund may be able to tell investors it has returned part of their capital. The second clock belongs to the company: each interest date, each loan maturity, each refinancing decision, each quarter in which sales must meet a larger fixed claim. The lender has priced that second clock, but the price cannot make the future certain. The transaction has changed who holds the cash while uncertainty resolves.

One might say that the money is already on both sides of the accounts: a dividend here, a loan there. That is true on a complete set of books. Yet the people who act on those books do not all stand at the same time or in the same place. The owner can invest or distribute the cash today. The employer will decide next year whether to raise wages. A creditor holding an older loan may find that the company now owes another lender as well. Employees and older lenders did not necessarily consent to the new distribution, even if the new lender knowingly accepted its risk.

The simplest accusation says that every such payout is extraction and every later failure proves it. KB Toys cannot carry that weight. The opposite defense says that willing lenders and visible debt make the dates irrelevant. That fails, too. The loan can be correctly recorded while its distribution changes what the company is able to do when the next shock arrives. To find out whether this is more than a morality tale attached to one bankruptcy, we need to look beyond the toy store.

### The firms chosen for the payout

Abhishek Bhardwaj, Abhinav Gupta, and Sabrina Howell assembled a large study of private equity buyouts and subsequent dividend recaps. Their dataset contains 61,628 U.S. buyout deals, of which 1,572 had an identified recap. That is 2.6 percent of deals, representing 9.3 percent of buyout volume. The recap is not what happens to every company. It tends to happen to larger firms that look healthy enough for lenders to advance more money.

That selection is an important defense. A company with rising profits can borrow on better terms, return cash to its owners, and still have years of growth ahead. The study found that recap targets, before the new borrowing, were generally stronger than comparison firms. Looking at the later bankruptcy rate of recap firms against the rate of all other buyouts without accounting for this selection would obscure what the added debt did.

The researchers used an unusual route into the question. Some private equity sponsors had relationships with banks that happened to be underwriting more collateralized loan obligations, pools that buy leveraged loans. When that channel offered cheaper credit, a sponsor was more likely to recap a portfolio company. Comparing otherwise similar firms around those credit-supply openings gave the authors a way to estimate the effect of the extra borrowing rather than simply the traits of companies selected for it.

In their data, a typical recap increased the company's total debt by 84 percent. The authors' preferred causal estimate found a 31-percentage-point increase in the chance of bankruptcy within the following six years for firms whose recap was induced by that credit opening. This is a striking estimate. It describes the firms tipped into a recap by that source of credit, not the raw difference between all recap and non-recap firms or a universal rate for every deal. The direction answers the defense's central question: the company can look strong enough to borrow and still become materially more fragile because it borrowed to pay owners.

The study also followed people beyond the original deal table. It found lower wage growth among employees at firms that survived long enough to be observed, and falling prices for loans that creditors already held around the recap. The creditor-price result is observational; it does not identify a causal effect as firmly as the study's main bankruptcy design. Still, it shows where financial markets looked for a bearer: an old loan became less attractive when new debt arrived. A gain booked for one claim can be a deterioration in another.

There is a result that complicates the story in a useful way. Recaps increased the chance of some excellent outcomes too, including public offerings and very strong revenue growth among surviving firms. More leverage widens the range of possible endings. The question is not whether every recap sends a healthy company to ruin. It is whether the reward from pulling cash forward goes to the same people, on the same dates, as the additional risk. The evidence says that often it does not.

Return to KB Toys with that wider picture in mind. The retailer is an observed transaction and an observed bankruptcy, separated by twenty-two months. It is not a controlled experiment. The study supplies the broader test: even after accounting for the tendency to choose strong targets, extra debt paid out to investors increased later distress in the researchers' setting. The toy store gives the clock a face. The study tells us why the clock is a mechanism rather than a coincidence chosen for a dramatic chapter opening.

### Selling the future

A dividend recap is one way to bring future receipts into the present. Asset-backed securities are another. A lender can assemble credit-card, auto-loan, or other receivables, place them in a financing structure, and sell securities whose payments depend on collections from that pool. The originator receives funding now; investors receive claims on later cash flows.

This can finance lending and distribute risk productively. It also forces us to keep two questions apart: how much cash came forward, and who is left with a loss if the future collections disappoint. The first can be measured at issuance. The second depends on the pool, the contracts, retained interests, guarantees, and the outcome over time.

In its 2025 Capital Markets Fact Book, SIFMA reported $388.1 billion of U.S. asset-backed securities issued in 2024, 43.3 percent more than in 2023. Separately, a credit-market report drawing on PitchBook data counted about $81 billion of institutional leveraged loans used for dividend recaps that year. These are different instruments and different measures. Neither number is a tally of losses or of dollars delivered to sponsors after fees. Together, they show the scale on which financial markets can exchange present cash for claims on future payment.

The familiar household version is selling next year's harvest before it grows. The comparison is useful until it isn't. A farmer who sells forward may still bear weather risk through the sale terms, insurance, and obligations to deliver. A securitization divides risks among investors and other parties according to a detailed legal structure. A dividend recap sends money to owners while a company retains debt. A market total cannot tell us the residual bearer. To find that person, we must open the specific record and follow the claim.

One record gave a rare view of what happens when the rules for drawing that record change.

### The morning the boundary moved

On January 1, 2010, Bank of America adopted new U.S. accounting guidance for consolidating certain special-purpose entities. Its 2010 annual report says the adoption added a net $100.4 billion in assets and $106.7 billion in liabilities to the consolidated balance sheet. Of the added assets, $69.7 billion came from credit-card trusts and $30.7 billion from other special-purpose entities, including multi-seller conduits. These were existing arrangements brought inside a newly drawn reporting boundary. The bank did not receive a hundred billion dollars of fresh customer deposits that morning.

The bank also recorded a $10.8 billion increase in its allowance for loan and lease losses, mostly related to credit-card receivables. The cumulative accounting effect included a $6.2 billion after-tax charge to retained earnings. The change affected regulatory capital: the annual report says its risk-weighted assets rose and its Tier 1 capital ratios fell when the new guidance and related final rule were applied.

Read those entries as two different events. The $100.4 billion is the amount of assets newly consolidated, not an amount lost. The allowance and retained-earnings charge concern expected credit losses and the cumulative effect of adoption; they are not interchangeable with the gross assets. The bank's actual credit performance still had to unfold afterward. The distinction is essential because the size of a door opening does not tell you how much damage was behind it.

Why, then, does the episode belong in a chapter about time? Before the rule change, a reader could find information about securitized loans in notes and other reports. After it, the same arrangements entered the main balance sheet and changed capital calculations. A rule selected *when* a class of exposure had to meet the ordinary reader's and regulator's primary view. It also changed which measures of strength were calculated from that view. The existing economics did not wait for January 2010 to exist; the mandatory consolidated picture did.

Picture a theater with a side room visible through a doorway. The people and chairs in that room are real, and an attentive visitor can look in. Then the wall is removed and the side room becomes part of the main floor plan. The building has not gained a room. Every count that starts from the main floor now includes it. The metaphor stops at the doorway: accounting consolidation follows control, risks, and contractual interests, while a building plan follows walls. But the change in the reader's default field of view is real in both.

This is also where the comforting claim that disclosure settles everything runs out of force. A disclosed footnote can inform a careful reader. It does not automatically carry the same weight as a balance-sheet line used in ratios, limits, and capital requirements. The bank's report itself records a change in those requirements. Placement and timing of an entry shape who must confront it before making the next decision.

The Bank of America event is not the later outcome of the KB Toys recap, and the 2024 issuance figures do not explain the bank's 2010 allowance. They are three windows onto one financial capacity: expected future payments can be turned into present money; the account of the remaining obligations can sit in different places and enter the central picture at different times. The job of the reader is to follow each transaction's own dates, amounts, and bearers before drawing the wider conclusion.

The Ledger's first financial power is therefore neither a secret formula nor a universal fraud. It is the power to choose when a claim becomes cash for one party and when its corresponding obligation constrains another. Dates are part of the distribution of value.

KB Toys' court record leaves a further clue. Big Lots held a note from the original sale. The court described that note as an obligation of a holding company whose only asset was stock in another holding company, and whose claim would rank behind creditors of the operating businesses in bankruptcy. The payout's timing explains why cash could leave before the business failed. The chain of companies explains where a later creditor had to stand to seek payment.

The next move of the Ledger is to draw that chain.
