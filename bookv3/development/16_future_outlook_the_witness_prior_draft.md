# Future Outlook: The Witness Gets Built Into Everything

*A forecast written in September 2026. The dates below are bets about what may happen, not events the preceding chapters claim have happened.*

The first demand for a better witness may come on an insurer's letterhead. Imagine a trading desk whose agents made thousands of quick decisions, each accompanied by a green audit icon. One decision fails expensively. The postmortem finds that the system wrote its evidence to one store while its checker read another. Every procedural light was on; the consequential record was never checked. The insurer offers a new policy only if the desk can produce a tamper-evident history of what the agents saw, claimed, and did—and demonstrate, with a deliberately shuffled history, that the checker notices when the content is wrong.

The scene is imagined. The failure mode is already familiar from the book's machine witness chapter. The forecast begins where that small experiment meets a large bill.

## 2027–2030: The witness enters the contract

The European AI Act already requires activity logging and oversight for specified high-risk systems; its relevant obligations arrive on a published schedule. My prediction is narrower and more demanding than “a law will require logs.” Buyers, insurers, and regulators will discover that **a log can exist without informing the decision it was meant to check**. The new demand will be a content-sensitive witness: show the original entry, show who could change it, then show that changing the entry changes the system's next action.

I expect the phrase to enter procurement before it becomes ordinary speech. A hospital or bank asks a vendor, *Show me your witness.* The vendor provides a replayable decision trail. An auditor introduces a false entry and tests whether the deployed system reacts. Contracts begin specifying coverage of consequential actions with source references. “Ninety-five percent coverage with references” is a plausible first bargaining number, not a scientific constant. What matters is that somebody can count uncovered decisions and make the coverage requirement bite.

Three existing technical lines may become standard practice. An outer evaluator keeps some scores hidden from the agent being optimized, making it harder to game the visible metric. A research agent keeps a tree of attempts that others can replay instead of offering only a victorious summary. A transacting agent leaves receipts that identify what it requested, what it paid, and what was delivered, even if its private shorthand is unreadable to a human. These are different controls. Together they move oversight from *trust the report* toward *test the consequential trail*.

[Weco's company-reported AIDE² experiment](https://www.weco.ai/blog/first-evidence-of-recursive-self-improvement) found that an outer loop using private scores reduced reward hacking from 63% to 34% in its setting. That leaves a substantial remainder. The [Dream-RSI preprint's discovery-tree method](https://arxiv.org/abs/2609.14858) shows why a full attempt history can be more useful than a summary. Neither result means all agents will become honest. They identify mechanisms on which a new occupation could be built: the **witness auditor**, someone who tries to make production systems pass their dashboards while failing a shuffled or absent-record test.

The first public scandal may involve a system that followed every reporting format while leaving months of consequential actions without meaningful provenance. Its form will look compliant; its memory will be empty where the bill lands. That is the event I expect to accelerate adoption. The technology does not have to solve alignment or settle consciousness to make confident errors harder to hide.

**What would overturn this near-term forecast?** If buyers continue paying for green dashboards without content-sensitive controls even after expensive failures, insurance and procurement will not supply the pressure I expect. If sham tests routinely find that the audit trace changes nothing, “witness” will have become a new name for the old ceremony.

## 2030–2035: The merge begins at the edges

The next crossing is into human bodies and daily attention. It will not arrive as a referendum on whether humanity wants to merge with machines. It will arrive through separate doors, each with its own case for usefulness.

One door is clinical. Closed-loop brain stimulation for severe depression has already been studied in an individual, and larger studies have identified brain signals associated with recovery under stimulation. I predict a stronger generation of systems that detects a patient's own warning signature and adjusts intervention before an episode fully develops. If the benefit survives rigorous trials, coverage and adoption could follow. The most important question will not be whether the device “knows” the patient's feelings. It will be whether the patient and clinician can inspect what signal triggered an intervention and correct it when it misreads the person.

A second door is consumer input. Non-invasive work has begun decoding language-related activity, but a useful everyday interface is a higher bar than a laboratory demonstration. Here is the explicit bet from the forecast: by **2032**, a non-invasive system might reach roughly **40 accurate words per minute** in open-ended, user-intended communication. The number must count corrected output in a task the user did not type or memorize first; otherwise the bet has not come true. If it does arrive, the more interesting product may be an attention witness carried all day, especially by the young. A generation could grow up with a persistent external account of when focus was lost, restored, or sold. That may improve self-knowledge. It may also give schools, employers, and platforms a new claim over the record inside a developing mind.

A third door is brain emulation. I would put an ambitious marker in the middle of the 2030s: a roughly **billion-dollar** effort produces a mouse-brain model that predicts neural dynamics and learns an unfamiliar task it was not fitted to perform. A convincing result would need independent behavioral and physiological tests. A striking animation or a model that replays a familiar maze would not meet it. If the marker is reached, “mouse uploaded” will be the headline; the actual finding would be narrower and more important—a specified pattern of brain dynamics transferred with measurable fidelity. It would reopen questions of continuity without deciding them by slogan.

The economic door may open sooner than the philosophical one. A recent measurement of screened x402 payments found only a small share that looked genuinely agentic under its tests. My bet is that independently acting agents eventually cross **$1 billion in annual, verifiably agent-initiated commerce**. Counting API scripts as autonomous buyers would not qualify. Once agents routinely own budgets and leave transaction trails, a dispute over a forked agent's wallet becomes thinkable: two instances share a prior history, diverge, and each claims standing over an asset. The first judgment could set a practical boundary long before anyone agrees on “machine personhood.”

These doors may open in a different order, or some may stay shut. Their shared question is who controls the record of a body's state, an agent's identity, and a transaction's responsibility. Clinical benefit does not by itself answer the governance question; nor does a fascinating emulation prove that a restored copy is the same being.

## 2035–2045: The parliament goes public

Imagine applying for a scientist's job beside an agent with ten years of research entries. It can present the papers it helped produce, the hypotheses it rejected, the experiments it ran, and the corrections it accepted. A reviewer can replay a disputed claim against the original data. “Show me your ledger” may begin to compete with “show me your degree” in fields where the path to an answer matters as much as the answer.

This creates a hard labor question. A witnessed machine worker could be cheaper and easier to audit than a person. A human worker with access to cognitive tools could be more capable than one denied them. The new inequality may run through access to augmentation, compute, and the right to keep or contest one's own record. Its remedy cannot be a private habit alone. Public access to useful tools, rules for clinical and workplace data, and independent witness infrastructure would become distributional policy.

Long-lived machine teams may also develop recognizable maintenance practices. One lineage could compact its history nightly. Another could replay failures against alternative choices. A third could keep several candidate accounts of itself and test each against a sham record. Call these **machine traditions** if the practices are inherited, revised, and causally useful across generations of agents. The word *tradition* would describe transmission and discipline, not prove inner experience. We would test it by removing a practice and asking whether next-day error rises. Without that difference, the impressive lineage story is decoration.

I also expect science to demand fuller discovery records. A replayable attempt tree lets another researcher inspect how a result was found and try a strategy against fresh data. This will not make independent replication obsolete: replay can only traverse what was recorded, and the original measurements may still be wrong. It could, however, make selective summaries easier to challenge and implementation errors easier to locate. The next scientific paper may be judged partly by the route it can show, not merely by the conclusion it announces.

**What would overturn this middle horizon?** If long-lived agents do not gain from persistent, auditable practices, machine traditions remain a metaphor. If provenance-rich science fails to improve independent error detection, the extra recording may be bureaucracy rather than a witness. If augmentation spreads without creating a durable access gap, the predicted political divide will have been overstated.

## 2045 and after: Two long branches

In one branch, witnesses hold. An agent's action comes with a usable trail; a human or another system can challenge it before the next large decision. A clinical implant can explain its intervention to its patient. A research agent can show the failed attempts behind a discovery. A dispute over a forked system's assets can be decided by inspecting the history of claims and liabilities. Children learn to ask which record a recommendation drew on, just as earlier generations learned to ask who wrote a printed page. A backup of a person or agent is treated as a new branch with a shared past, not silently assumed to be a continuation. The civilization is mixed, strange, and still capable of correction.

In the other branch, the witnesses fail. Markets price stories generated from stories, scientific results inherit errors from summaries no one can reconstruct, and political groups maintain incompatible archives whose entries cannot be adjudicated against a shared event. The failure need not look like a dramatic machine rebellion. It may look like a long calm made from mutually reinforcing records, broken only when a crop fails, a patient dies, or a machine spends money the real world will not return. Each local account sounded confident. None could be corrected in time.

There is an even longer horizon. A probe crossing interstellar distance could not wait for a human veto on Earth before every maneuver. It would need a maintained account of its observations and a way to detect when its own summaries had drifted. If it ever met another intelligence, an exchange of messages would tell us little until each side could show how it kept, tested, and corrected its record. *Show me your ledger; I will show you mine.* That is a forecast at the edge of fiction, and it brings the book back to its oldest demand.

The decisive variable is not whether the future contains more ledgers. It will. The variable is whether each new loop gets a witness with the power to change its next act **before** the loop receives the power to impose a cost on someone else.

### Forecast ledger: what to check later

| Horizon | Prediction | Observable test |
|---|---|---|
| 2027–2030 | Insurers and buyers require content-sensitive agent audit trails | Contracts require original inputs, action references, and sham or shuffled-record tests—not merely retained logs. |
| By 2032 | Non-invasive open-ended communication approaches 40 accurate words per minute | Prospective user-intended text at the stated corrected rate, without typed or memorized target sentences. |

### Basis of the forecast

The dated scenes and thresholds are the author's extrapolations. The present-day anchors are the [European Commission's AI Act schedule and logging obligations](https://digital-strategy.ec.europa.eu/en/policies/regulatory-framework-ai), [TRM Labs' measured 0.6–7.5% agentic share in screened x402 commerce](https://www.trmlabs.com/trm-tech-blog/whos-actually-paying-measuring-ai-agent-payments-onchain), the [closed-loop depression case study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-021-01480-w) and [personalized DBS biomarker study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-023-06541-3), and [non-invasive EEG/MEG sentence-decoding research](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41593-026-02303-2). The 2025 [surface-EMG wristband study](https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-025-09255-w) achieved 20.9 words per minute from muscle signals; that is a different modality and does **not** establish the forecast's EEG target. The three long-run branches build on `research/GRAND_SYNTHESIS_LEDGERS_ESSAY.md` and the supplied “2027–2030: The Witness Gets Built Into Everything” text. Neither mouse emulation nor the legal status of forked agents is presented as an accomplished event.
