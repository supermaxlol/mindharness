# Chapter 4: The Company That Could Disappear

The note had a name on it. That was the problem.

When Big Lots sold KB Toys in 2000, it received cash and a $45 million promise to be paid later. The promise was a *pay-in-kind* note: interest could accumulate rather than arrive as cash each year. The company that issued it was Havens Corners Corporation, or HCC. On a chart of the toy business, HCC looked comfortably connected. Above it sat KB Holdings. Below it sat another holding company, then the operating subsidiaries with the stores, inventory, and customers.

The chart made all of these boxes look like one enterprise. The note named one box. HCC's only asset, the Delaware court later explained, was stock in the holding company below it. If the operating subsidiaries could not pay their own creditors, value would not climb the chain to HCC merely because all the boxes carried the same toy-store identity. Big Lots had accepted a claim that was subordinate to creditors nearer the stores.

The first question was *which company owed the money?*

In 2002, the operating subsidiaries borrowed and sent part of the proceeds upward. About $120 million was distributed to the buyout sponsor, affiliates, managers, and others, according to the court's account. The calendar told us when the money left. Now follow its address. The operating businesses took on the borrowing. The cash passed through HCC and up to KB Holdings, where shares were bought from owners. Big Lots' note stayed an HCC obligation.

When KB Toys entered Chapter 11 in January 2004, the note had grown to about $57 million with accrued interest. Big Lots sued the Bain affiliates and individuals, alleging that the refinancing had drained value from the business and left its note unpaid. It asked a Delaware court to treat the injury as one for which Big Lots could pursue those defendants directly.

The court dismissed the suit. Several of the asserted wrongs, it held, were injuries to the company as a whole. Claims for that injury belonged to the bankruptcy estate, which acts for creditors collectively, rather than to one creditor trying to recover first. Other pleaded claims failed on their own terms. The dismissal closed this attempted route to the recipients of the cash; it left the estate's collective claims to the bankruptcy process.

That ruling served a real purpose. If the largest, best-lawyered unsecured creditor could take a company-wide claim out of bankruptcy and collect for itself, every smaller unsecured creditor would be left behind. The rule protected the collective process. It also closed Big Lots' attempted route to the people who had received the earlier cash.

### The name on the key

Imagine a corridor of locked rooms in one building. The sign outside says **KB Toys**. Your key says **HCC**. It opens the HCC room; it does not open every door bearing the same sign. In the HCC room you find shares in a room farther down the corridor. Before you can turn those shares into money, people with claims directly against that lower room must be paid.

The physical picture helps only up to a point. Corporate law is not a set of unbreakable doors. A guarantee can give a creditor a key to a parent. A lien can attach to a named asset. Insurance can fund a claim. A court can set aside a fraudulent transfer, or in some circumstances disregard a company's separate personality. Bankruptcy law may gather claims and property that individual lawsuits cannot. The contract, statute, and judgment decide which passages are open. A logo does not.

This is why the familiar statement “the company has plenty of money” may tell a claimant almost nothing. *Which company?* Does that company owe this debt? Did it guarantee another's debt? What asset backs the promise? What happens to the promise if the operating company fails? A consolidated balance sheet can add the rooms together for investors. A court still needs the legal route from claimant to asset.

Big Lots was no powerless bystander to its original bargain. The court emphasized that it was a sophisticated seller and had no contractual right to block the 2002 refinancing. It had taken a deeply subordinated note. Perhaps this was a risk Big Lots priced and accepted. That defense explains why its note stood behind other claims. It also makes the architecture plain: a negotiated promise can stay at one address while cash moves among related addresses. The contract gives the creditor a position in the chain, not ownership of the whole chain.

Now reverse the operation. Instead of leaving a creditor at an existing address, create an address for claims already brought against the business. The next question is what travels into that room with the claims.

### A company built to hold the claims

Johnson & Johnson's consumer-products business had faced talc lawsuits for years and disputed that its products caused the alleged injuries. For claimants, a second question stood before any award: which entity would answer, and through which forum?

In October 2021, J&J reorganized its consumer business through a divisional merger under Texas law. The old company ended. One successor, New Consumer, received the operating business and most of its assets. Another, LTL Management, received essentially all of the old company's talc-related liabilities. Two days later, on October 14, LTL sought Chapter 11 protection. People who had brought talc claims would now face a company created to carry those claims into bankruptcy.

The brands had one address; the claims had another. But the plan also built a passage between them. LTL received cash, an interest in a royalty business, and, most importantly, a funding agreement. Under that agreement, J&J and New Consumer were jointly responsible for paying LTL's qualifying talc costs up to the value of New Consumer. The court described the right as worth roughly $61.5 billion at the time of filing, with a floor tied to New Consumer's value at the split. That payment right changed what the new address meant.

Follow the promise through three questions. Who could demand payment? LTL could. Who had to supply it? Both J&J and New Consumer were responsible. What happened after the money arrived? LTL did not have to repay it. Outside bankruptcy, the permitted uses included talc costs and ordinary expenses; inside bankruptcy, they included administration and a claimant trust. The conditions were limited. This was a contractual route to cash, rather than a hope that a generous parent might send help.

The distinction matters because a corporate chart can conceal resources in either direction. A liability box can look empty while holding a valuable claim on another box. Count only the cash inside it and you miss the promise. Count the whole group's assets and you miss the conditions under which the box can demand them. The Third Circuit read the payment right and the strength of the parties behind it. A company seeking shelter had to account for the bridge it had built to reassure everyone that its obligations would be funded.

The Third Circuit used that right to turn the company's argument back on itself. LTL had asked bankruptcy to protect it from the cost and disorder of mass litigation. The court asked whether the debtor actually faced the kind of financial distress that justified Chapter 11. A valuable, reliable payment right against J&J and New Consumer made the answer no on the record before it. In 2023 the court ordered the first LTL bankruptcy dismissed.

The company holding liabilities was separate. The funding promise made the separation permeable. The bridge intended to support a bankruptcy resolution became the reason the court closed its entrance: LTL had resources sufficient to defeat its claim to the required financial distress. A drawn boundary had met an enforceable passage and a court willing to read both.

There was still a valuable prize in the reorganization: control of the route. A mass-tort bankruptcy can gather current and future claims, pause individual suits, value disputed claims, and, if a lawful plan is confirmed, channel payment through a trust. Those powers can help pay many people fairly when separate lawsuits threaten to consume the common pool. They can also replace a claimant's path to an operating company or jury with a slower, collective procedure. Moving the forum is a distributional act even when a funding agreement leaves substantial money on the table.

The next case shows that a similar corridor can remain closed to individual suits while that collective procedure continues.

### The door that stayed shut

Georgia-Pacific had faced asbestos claims arising from products sold decades earlier. In 2017 it used a Texas divisional merger to create New GP and Bestwall. New GP received most of the operating company's assets. Bestwall received the asbestos liabilities, about $32 million in cash, litigation-related contracts and insurance, a plaster business valued at roughly $145 million at filing, and a funding agreement under which New GP would cover specified expenses and, if necessary, a bankruptcy trust. Bestwall filed Chapter 11 that November.

Some claimants continued to name New GP in asbestos suits. Bestwall asked the bankruptcy court to halt those suits while it handled claims in one proceeding. The court granted a preliminary injunction. In 2023, the Fourth Circuit affirmed the injunction against suits involving the nondebtor affiliate. Its majority said the parallel claims against New GP could affect Bestwall's bankruptcy and that the funding agreement could make New GP's assets available through the estate for approved claims.

To understand the disagreement, follow a possible payment around the loop. Bestwall had agreed to indemnify New GP for asbestos liabilities. If a claimant won against New GP, New GP could seek reimbursement from Bestwall. That demand could affect the bankruptcy estate. But New GP had also agreed to fund Bestwall when the relevant resources were insufficient. In the dissent's account, New GP could supply Bestwall with the money Bestwall would use to reimburse New GP. The claimant's lawsuit was being connected to a bankruptcy through agreements drawn by the companies themselves.

The majority saw an estate that could still be affected. Bestwall's own resources came first; New GP's funding served as a backstop. Even an exchange in which New GP supplied the money could change Bestwall's liabilities and the administration of the case. The dissent challenged both the claimed economic effect and the use of constructed relationships to establish the court's reach. These were rival readings of an actual contract, with a different next step for claimants depending on which reading prevailed.

The majority's reading prevailed. The path of suing New GP separately remained blocked by the injunction while the bankruptcy process went on. The promise helped make resources available through the estate, and the order made that estate the route claimants had to use for the enjoined suits.

In August 2025 the Fourth Circuit also affirmed the denial of a separate jurisdictional dismissal challenge. The arrangement had survived two challenges: individual suits against New GP remained redirected, and the bankruptcy continued on the questions presented. The funding promise supplied a passage to resources; the procedure selected the passage through which claimants could seek them. No final trust or individual recovery was established by those appellate rulings.

LTL and Bestwall met different legal tests in different courts. One funding bridge helped defeat entry into bankruptcy. The other supported a route that kept individual affiliate suits outside it. The decisive question is what a court allows the written boundary and its bridge to do.

### The vote that did not become a voice

J&J tried again. Its 2024 Red River Talc plan proposed roughly $9 billion to resolve ovarian and other gynecological cancer claims. It sought votes before the bankruptcy filing, aiming to arrive with the support needed for a prepackaged settlement. The new liability company and its parent presented a plan that, if confirmed, would route claims through a trust and release some claims against others.

One settlement offered finality across tens of thousands of disputed claims and a share for future claimants. Many claimants and firms supported it. The court had to decide whether the recorded support could authorize the plan to bind the people whose rights it changed.

The bankruptcy judge examined the record of the votes. He found serious solicitation and tabulation problems, including too little time for thousands of claimants to make a choice and difficulties establishing who had authority to vote for whom. The court could not certify that the required class had accepted the plan. It also found other barriers to confirmation, including improper nonconsensual releases of claims against parties outside bankruptcy and an overbroad attempt to channel claims against retailers. On March 31, 2025, it denied confirmation and dismissed Red River's case for cause.

The judge's short sentence belongs at the center of this book: “Votes are voices.” A mark in a voting ledger can authorize a settlement that binds people. If a lawyer's mark does not reliably represent a client's decision, the page may be full while the court has not heard the people whose claims would be changed. The vote count had crossed into authority. A tally could not supply the voices it failed to carry.

J&J said it would return to the tort system rather than appeal. It also announced that it would reverse approximately $7 billion it had reserved for the proposed bankruptcy resolution. That accounting change was neither cash paid to claimants nor a judgment that the injuries had vanished. It was the company's next entry after the chosen route failed. The pending claims still needed a forum and a result.

Here the Ledger's power becomes visible at human scale. A divisional merger chooses the name on the defendant's door. A funding agreement chooses what can pass between rooms. An injunction chooses where a claimant may speak. A ballot chooses whose consent can make a new plan binding. Each written choice changes the next move available to someone else.

### Follow the passage, not the logo

The financial report draws a different map of the rooms. In Chapter 3, an accounting rule brought Bank of America's existing securitization vehicles into its main balance sheet. Other bank filings disclose a *maximum exposure to loss* from vehicles they do not consolidate. That number estimates what the **bank** could lose from its interests and commitments to a vehicle. It is not, by itself, the amount a creditor of that vehicle can legally collect from the bank. Accounting consolidation changes reported assets, liabilities, and ratios. A guarantee, lien, contract, statute, or judgment determines legal recourse. A bank's risk of losing money and a claimant's right to reach that bank are different entries.

Three reassuring shortcuts have now failed. “There is money in the group” could not turn Big Lots' HCC note into a claim against every company in the toy-store chain. “The liability company is separate, so there is no money” missed LTL's enforceable funding right. “There is a funding promise, so the claimant's position is secure” missed Bestwall's change of forum and Red River's attempt to bind claimants through a disputed vote.

What survives those tests is a question we can actually investigate: who can make which payer supply money, on what conditions, through which procedure? An empty company means zero recovery only when no enforceable passage leads to assets, insurance, or another payer. A funded company can still leave its claimants fighting over the route. The entry and the power to use it must be read together.

Corporate boundaries let people invest, lend, insure, and reorganize without every failure consuming every related enterprise. A collective process can protect later claimants from the first plaintiffs taking everything. These are powerful reasons to draw a boundary—and powerful reasons to ask who draws it. The rule that prevents one creditor from jumping the queue can decide which assets enter the queue and which route every creditor must follow.

The Ledger has learned to choose an address and the passages out of it. **The question is whether this claim can touch that money.** Big Lots held a real note. Talc claimants asserted causes of action. Even reaching the right defendant leaves another journey. A claim can be recognized in full and return as a fraction of the cash owed.
