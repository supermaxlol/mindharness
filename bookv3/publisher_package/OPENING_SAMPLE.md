# The Ledger Principle — Opening sample

Shehzad Ahmed

# Prologue: The Pattern That Borrows Stuff

A leaf enters a whirlpool at the bend of a river. It travels around twice, catches the outward current, and is gone. Another leaf takes its place. You could stand there all afternoon and watch the same shape hold while the water that first made it passed downstream.

A rock sits a few feet below it. The rock keeps most of its material and slowly loses its edge. The whirlpool borrows new material to keep its edge. Take away the current and there is no damaged whirlpool resting on the riverbed. There is no whirlpool.

Your body and mind persist more like the whirlpool.

In 1953, the physicist Paul Aebersold estimated that roughly **98 percent of the atoms in a person are replaced within a year**. It was a whole-body estimate; tissues and elements turn over at different rates. You can feel the movement without a laboratory. You drink, breathe, eat, shed, exhale. Material leaves. More arrives. Yet a street learned in childhood can still guide your feet. A sentence spoken thirty years ago can change your pulse today.

Where did that sentence wait?

Calling it a recording invites us to imagine an original tape in a protected cabinet. But memory faces a more specific problem than whole-body turnover. Much of the molecular machinery at the junctions between neurons is renewed on timescales far shorter than the memories it helps carry. The arrangement has to remain usable as its components change.

Connections strengthen or weaken. Cells build and replace proteins. Activity returns to some paths and lets others fade. Neuroscientists investigate how these processes sustain the effects of learning over time. A past encounter survives by changing the way a later encounter unfolds.

Think of a wrong turn on the way home. The error does not have to remain as an untouched picture of a corner. It has to make you hesitate there next time. What persists is a difference in what you can do.

Here the river image reaches its limit. The whirlpool keeps its form without remembering the leaf. You maintain a body that carries a history into new situations. You can compare the old warning with the road in front of you. You can also keep obeying it after the danger has passed. Maintenance preserves useful routes and practiced mistakes alike.

Then the pattern makes its great crossing. It leaves the body.

Imagine a house beside a river. After a flood, someone draws a short line on a wooden post to show how high the water reached. Years later, another person is deciding where to set the floor of a new house. The flood is gone. The person who drew the line is gone. The mark remains. The builder sets the floor above it.

One stranger paid for a discovery. Another inherits it without standing in the water.

The scratch can wait through silence. It can be copied, carried elsewhere, compared with other marks, and found by someone its maker never imagined. Experience has acquired a life longer than the life that acquired it.

Now read the mark again. It records the height of **one flood**. The builder takes it for the highest flood the river could ever bring. Nothing beside the line says “one year only.” The trace is faithful; the conclusion is wrong. When a higher flood comes, the family living in the house pays for a condition the record left out.

Everyone used the same mark. They did not use the same meaning.

A date beside the line would help. So would older marks, another village's account, or a person who remembers water above that height. But the correction has to reach the builder before the floor is set. A warning added to a file nobody opens can preserve a perfect account of a preventable loss.

A maintained record becomes a **ledger** when someone consults it to decide what happens next. It can be a line on a post, a recipe, a map, an account, a court docket, a story, or a machine's saved state. Their powers differ. A recipe advises; a court order can compel. Their shared capacity is to make yesterday available to act on today.

Did the line merely report the flood? It selected a place, a height, and an event. It left out a time span. Its reader supplied an interpretation, chose a floor height, and placed a family above the water she expected. The record helped determine who would stand in the next flood. Yesterday’s observation had become one of tomorrow’s causes.

The builder still made a choice. She could have measured more or asked around. Yet the mark put an observation in front of someone who had never seen the flood. Alter its label and you can alter her choice. A ledger needs no mind or motive of its own to acquire power. It needs keepers, readers, and decisions that continue to consult it.

More information can improve the arrangement. Fill the post with dates. Add photographs. Build a database of every river rise. Each step also places more authority somewhere: with the person who measures, the office that keeps the series, the institution that chooses which height an insurer or lender will use. A complete-looking record may be harder to challenge than a scratch on wood. Someone still has to get a warranted correction into the decision.

The Ledger Principle is this: **Systems that persist do it by maintaining a checkable record, and whoever writes the record decides who pays.**

Here, persistence means carrying learned history into a later action. Checkability means a route by which an entry can meet evidence, be corrected, and change that action. The crucial political question is who gets to use the route. A keeper may inspect a record closely while the people governed by it struggle to see the page. Control of a record includes control of whose challenge is allowed to count.

The gift is immense. A child inherits tools, numbers, and stories that thousands of dead strangers spent their lives improving. She can begin beyond the limits they paid to reach. She also inherits their omissions. A faulty instruction travels as readily as a useful one. An old judgment can arrive in a new life with all its authority intact.

Civilization makes those judgments enforceable. An entry can say who owns a field, which company owes a debt, or whose injury qualifies for payment. Its placement can let one party collect today while another waits years to discover what remains. The record distributes the bill before anyone opens an argument about fairness.

Culture brings the same power closer. A fable can give a child a model of betrayal before she has met it. The words are portable; her memories and imagination complete them. Later, the remembered story can help her notice a danger—or make an innocent person resemble its villain. The record changes its reader, and the changed reader chooses what to record next.

Now machines write and consult records for other machines. They can pass an efficient code to successors, generate the material from which successors learn, and inspect entries faster than a human clerk. A green light can glow even when the checker read the wrong file. More speed gives the next decision less time to encounter a human objection.

The scale changes. Four questions keep their force: **Who keeps the record? Who can check and amend it? What decision does it guide? Who pays when it is wrong?**

You can follow them through a kitchen, a court, a bank filing, or a software log. They let us test the Ledger's power where it becomes a consequence someone has to live with.

Return to the river. The whirlpool cannot care whether the leaf survives. The flood mark cannot care whether the builder understands it. People can care. We made records so that what one person learned could reach another person in time. Their greatest promise and deepest danger begin at that crossing.

How does a discovery survive its discoverer—and what must the next person learn before the inheritance becomes useful?

# Chapter 1: The Mark That Outlived Its Maker

The first move earns the bee nothing.

Inside a clear puzzle box is sugar. A red tab opens the way to it, but the red tab will not move until a blue tab has been pushed aside. Moving blue pays nothing. A bee working alone has every reason to give up at that point. The first action brings no reward, and the sweet solution stays hidden behind the second.

In a 2024 experiment, researchers trained bumblebees to solve this sequence. Even the demonstrators needed help at first: the researchers temporarily rewarded the blue step, then removed that reward. After training, an inexperienced bee could watch a demonstrator. Five of fifteen observers went on to open the box without ever receiving sugar for moving blue.

What did the successful learners get to see?

Some trained bees opened the box by squeezing past both tabs in one continuous turn. Five of ten observers paired with those demonstrators learned. Other demonstrators pushed the blue tab, moved around, then pushed red; none of the five observers paired with them learned. The researchers could not reduce this difference to the number of demonstrations.

The same box, the same reward, two ways of reaching it. In this experiment, the form of the example helped decide whether the solution traveled. A living teacher has to do more than succeed. Its action has to become legible to a learner.

Set this beside the simplest story people tell about human civilization. In that story, an animal discovers a trick, dies, and takes the trick with it. A human discovers a trick, passes it on, and history begins. The bee refuses its assigned part. A behavior that the observers did not work out alone passed between animals. The laboratory did not give the bees a library. It did show that human beings do not own the basic escape route from the death of a knowledgeable individual.

Pigeons press the point further. In another experiment, a pigeon flew home from the same release point twelve times, learning a route. It was then paired with a bird that did not know the route. After twelve flights together, the more experienced bird was replaced by a new one. The replacements continued through five generations of pairs. The later pairs flew more efficient routes than birds training alone or pairs whose membership never changed. Consecutive generations in a chain took similar paths, evidence that something about the route had traveled from bird to bird.

No pigeon wrote down a turn. The record was in the pair's behavior, in the corrections each flight left available to the next. The route improved as its carriers changed. If a ratchet is a tool that lets a wheel advance one tooth without slipping all the way back, this was a small cultural ratchet made of wings.

So the first easy explanation is gone. Culture, and even some cumulative improvement, cannot be defined simply as whatever humans do. The question worth asking is sharper: what can a pattern do when it no longer needs to be shown by the living body that knows it?

### The distance a lesson can travel

Stand beside someone who knows how to make bread. She presses the dough; you press yours. She adds a little water and tells you to stop when the mass yields in a certain way. She can see that your flour is drier than hers. She can interrupt before you make a mistake. If words fail, she can put your hand where the dough should be.

This is a powerful form of memory transfer. It has something a page will never have: a teacher who can respond to the learner in front of her. The bee's demonstrator and the pigeon's experienced partner offered narrower versions of the same advantage. Their actions were present, not merely reported.

The dependence is its weakness. The right teacher must meet the learner at the right time. If the teacher moves, dies, or has nobody interested in the lesson, a useful distinction can vanish. A later learner may receive most of a practice and miss the one small movement that makes it work. Oral traditions can preserve intricate skills over long spans; they are not failed books waiting to be invented. They do, however, require a chain of people and occasions in which the practice can be performed, noticed, and repaired.

Now put a recipe on a page. The page cannot touch the dough or notice that the oven runs hot. But it can sit unopened for years. It can cross a sea. Someone who never met the writer can place it beside another recipe, spot a difference, and try both. A person yet unborn can argue with its author in the margin.

That change separates the life of an instruction from the life of its maker. It also lets a learner compare absent teachers. In the pigeon experiment, the earlier route reached the newcomer through a living partner. A written route can be opened beside an older version. The disputed turn becomes an object that strangers can inspect together.

The earliest external records were often humbler than a recipe. Before writing as we know it, people in the ancient Near East used small clay counters of different shapes to track goods. Denise Schmandt-Besserat, the archaeologist who studied these objects, describes a progression from loose tokens to tokens held in sealed clay envelopes. Some envelopes carried impressions on the outside that corresponded to the counters inside. A surviving envelope from Susa, in present-day Iran, dates to about 3300 BCE.

Hold the object in your mind. The contents are inside a clay ball. The outside carries marks. To know what is being claimed about the contents, a reader need not break the envelope. A count has acquired an exterior. It can be checked against what was sealed away. A person can point to the same mark another person sees.

A clay tablet from Godin Tepe, dated to about 3100 BCE, makes the transition more striking. On Schmandt-Besserat's reading, its incised sign names oil and its circular and wedge impressions count thirty-three jars. The tablet does not preserve the jars. It preserves a claim about them. A holder of the tablet and a holder of the oil need not be standing together for the claim to remain available.

Spoken stories, tools, paths, and practiced skills had carried human histories long before those tablets. The clay added an inventory that could survive its counter. A later official could consult it. Two people could disagree over what arrived and point to a count made by someone who was no longer there.

To an untrained reader, thirty-three jars can look like a handful of dents. You can copy a Chinese character exactly without knowing what it says. A reader who has learned the language encounters the same strokes and finds meaning. Training supplies the difference. The tablet's reader needs its signs, the transaction they name, and the conditions under which the count was made. Meaning is not buried in the clay like oil in a jar. The durable surface gives later minds something from which to rebuild it.

An Egyptian stone lets us watch the rebuilding. In 196 BCE, a council of priests issued a decree concerning Ptolemy V. It appeared on stone in three scripts: hieroglyphs, demotic, and Greek. The two Egyptian scripts reached different readers; Greek served the administration. The inscription was made to be understood in its own world. Then the world of its readers changed. Hieroglyphic writing fell out of use, and the ability to read it disappeared. The marks survived their makers *and* the people trained to read them.

In 1799, soldiers at Rashid found a broken copy of the decree. Scholars could still read the Greek. That gave them a familiar account to place beside unfamiliar signs, much as a recipe in a language you know can help you approach the same recipe in one you do not. But the stone was no automatic dictionary. Thomas Young identified signs spelling the royal name Ptolemy. Jean-François Champollion tested the sound principle more widely, drawing also on Coptic, a later form of Egyptian. His 1822 announcement depended on earlier comparisons and surviving languages as well as on the stone.

The decree had not changed while it lay unread. What changed was the route between a mark and a trained mind. Even this famous parallel record needed living readers who could compare, make a wrong guess, and correct it. Its three scripts were not three identical rows of a modern translation table. A surviving surface made recovery possible; it did not perform the recovery.

### What the saved page leaves out

Return to the recipe. Imagine that its first line says to add a fixed amount of water. A later baker follows it and gets a sticky dough that collapses in the oven. The original cook is gone. The printed instruction remains perfectly legible. The later baker pays for the omitted condition in flour, fuel, and an evening's work.

What failed? It might have been the weather. It might have been a different flour. The cook might have relied on a gesture she never thought to write down. The page preserved the instruction faithfully, which is exactly why the failure can repeat. More accurate copying of an incomplete recipe produces more accurately repeated disappointment.

The baker writes a note in the margin: “Begin with less water when the flour already feels damp.” This is a small act, but it changes the inheritance. The next baker can use the original quantity, the amendment, and a fresh batch of dough to test both. If the new note is wrong, the next failure has a place to attach. The book now holds a dispute between attempts rather than a command from the absent cook.

The ink can survive unchanged while the practice fails. A recipe needs a reader with ingredients, skill, and judgment about the dough in front of her. The margin gives that judgment a way to reach the next reader.

Human beings are unusually good at making such surfaces matter. Children sometimes copy an adult's apparently unnecessary move even when a more direct route to the reward is visible. In a well-known puzzle-box experiment, chimpanzees were more likely to drop an irrelevant action when the box made its irrelevance clear; young children often continued to copy it. That can look like foolish obedience. It can also be a sensible wager for a novice: the adult may know a hidden condition, or the move may be part of a rule of the group. Copying the whole sequence keeps an unknown dependency alive until the learner can test it.

The wager has a price. If the adult's extra move is truly useless, faithful copying wastes effort. If the adult's explanation is wrong, it can narrow what the learner tries next. A culture needs enough fidelity to keep a rare working technique and enough freedom to notice when a transmitted step has ceased to earn its place. The recipe's margin is where those two needs meet.

A steel ratchet locks each tooth mechanically. Cultural inheritance can slip, split, or lock the wrong thing in place. A durable record lengthens the interval in which a loss can be noticed and repaired. Somebody still has to notice.

### Must we understand what we inherit?

Perhaps the page matters less than the mind that reads it. Humans build exceptional tools because they reason better about causes, this argument says. The recipe is just a convenient way for the clever baker to communicate what she already knows. If every improvement depends on a person who understands the whole mechanism, the true protagonist is still the individual brain.

Give people a wheel design and ask them to make it faster. Let the next learner inherit what the previous one tried. In Maxime Derex and his colleagues' experiment, the wheels improved across successive rounds, while participants' explicit explanations of how they worked did not improve along with them. Passing those explanations on did not noticeably accelerate progress. Inaccurate theories could even constrain later exploration. A working configuration traveled farther than its maker's account of why it worked.

You can ride a bicycle you could not manufacture, obey a medicine schedule without knowing its biochemistry, or use a mathematical method you could not have invented. What you inherit is real competence, assembled across many lives. The fact that you cannot reconstruct its whole discovery path does not make your action unreal.

François Osiurak and colleagues later ran a partial replication and found improvement accompanied by better technical understanding. Their result preserves an important role for reasoning. When the inherited design breaks, understanding can help a learner diagnose, adapt, or escape a bad theory. The question is whether every improvement requires that understanding first.

A 2026 study by Ali Seyhun Saral and colleagues separated those contributions more directly. Across two experiments with **900 participants**, learners inherited and refined solutions in tasks with a physical context, an abstract causal structure, or no useful causal structure. Improvement accumulated in all three conditions. Causal reasoning helped early progress; the physical context added no advantage over the abstract causal task. A successful choice could be preserved before its owner had an explanation worth passing on. Eureka need not precede improvement.

The same is true of the baker. She can use the written quantities before she understands dough. When the loaf fails, her ability to reason about water, flour, and heat becomes precious. The written page lets her begin ahead of the original cook's starting point; it does not spare her from thought. The copied artifact and the thinking reader make one working system.

A group of skilled teachers can keep a practice alive through demonstration. The page gives that group members who are dead, absent, or unknown. Communities can compare versions without bringing their oldest cooks together. A correction can wait beside the disputed step until a stranger finds it. The inheritance now carries responsibility with knowledge: an omitted condition can charge its cost to someone the writer will never meet.

The baker can write in a margin because she owns the book and knows how to read it. The next person may not. Clay marks need a code, a keeper, and a reader who knows where to find them. The strongest instruction can vanish from use if its language, tools, or teachers disappear. A tablet may last five thousand years while its working meaning disappears for most of that time.

The page has survived. Its readers may not. How many people must still know the signs? How many makers must be able to test the instruction? A record can outlive its last useful reading. To understand that loss, we have to enter the room of maps built around the single mark.

# Chapter 2: The Map Room

The point of the bone tool was still imaginable long after the last one vanished.

At sites in Tasmania, archaeologists found worked bone in older layers. In the later layers, the tools became less common, less varied, and then absent. At Rocky Cape, Joseph Henrich reported a ratio of stone to bone tools of three to one about seven thousand years ago. Several millennia later it was fifteen to one. By roughly three and a half thousand years ago, bone tools no longer appear in that record.

Picture the last lesson: a bone split, a point ground, a hand testing the edge. An apprentice sees a sequence she may one day need to show someone else. The archaeological layers preserve the objects. They leave us to reconstruct what happened to the lesson.

One account of the disappearance begins with a rising sea. Tasmania had been connected to mainland Australia. As the sea filled the Bass Strait, the island's people became more isolated from other groups. Henrich proposed that the resulting reduction in the *effective* pool of people from whom one could learn made some techniques harder to maintain. If a difficult procedure is copied imperfectly, the best model available to the next learner matters. With enough models and enough correction, an occasional mistake need not become the next generation's starting point. With fewer, a useful distinction can gradually disappear.

This is more unsettling than a sudden disaster. A tool can be lost while every maker remains a capable human being. No one has to decide to destroy the knowledge. The next apprentice merely inherits a slightly poorer demonstration, then gives a slightly poorer demonstration in turn.

Perhaps the lesson ended because the tool was no longer needed. Food, climate, materials, and other inventions change what deserves to be made. Archaeologist Krist Vaesen and his colleagues pressed this objection against the demographic account. Tasmanians continued to make canoes, baskets, shelters, and stone tools, including artifacts more demanding than the missing bone points. A record that counts the vanished point and omits the surviving work can manufacture a decline of its own. Henrich's model supplies a mechanism by which reduced contact could erode a technique. The objects alone do not establish that mechanism as the cause of this disappearance.

The objection changes what we must count. A census tells us how many people exist. A transmission map tells us whom an apprentice can watch, which example she selects, what materials she can use, and where she can take a failed attempt. The maintained route is the thing to explain.

### The paper plane that would not improve

Take a sheet of paper and try to make it fly as far as possible. At the next table, place the planes made by an earlier group. A new participant can study one, two, or four examples before making a plane for the next group. Which arrangement should produce the longest flight after several rounds?

The easy answer is four. More examples mean more designs and a better chance of encountering an excellent one. In a study of 543 participants, that first part happened: larger sets offered more variation and better available planes. But the expected inheritance did not follow. Plane distance improved across experimental generations in the condition where each participant saw one predecessor's plane. It did not improve across generations in the conditions with two or four models.

The experimenters examined the copies. Participants favored better planes, yet resemblance between one generation's designs and the next weakened as the number of models grew. The investigators proposed limited working memory and the burden of comparison as part of the explanation. More designs were available. The copies were less faithful. The missing step lay between access and inheritance.

Now imagine a crowded workshop where every maker shows a tool and announces how well it worked. Before your turn, the examples are taken away. The room holds good information; your hands must rebuild it from memory. You may borrow incompatible features from several designs or forget the one fold that mattered.

Bradley Walker and Nicolas Fay put the missing page inside a later experiment. Participants drew virtual arrowheads through ten trials. Each design received a score, but the rule that produced the score was hidden. In a group of six, a player watched the other five designs and scores one by one before making the next arrowhead. Groups of three saw fewer. If every example vanished before the player drew again, the larger group offered more ideas but no measured advantage over the smaller one.

In another condition, the same kinds of designs and scores stayed in a panel while the player drew the next arrowhead. Now the six-person groups outperformed the three-person groups. In those larger groups, performance improved across trials with the panel; it did not improve when everyone had to view all the examples without one. The researchers also found stronger copying of the high-scoring designs when the record was available. An additional maker helped once the next maker could return to an example at the moment of choice.

There is a rival explanation worth keeping: perhaps the panel mainly helped people filter attention, rather than combine several designs. In the researchers' separate first experiment, larger groups also benefited when a player could choose to inspect just one high-scoring model. The team did not directly measure recombination in the record condition. The panel was cleared after each trial, too; this was no proof of an archive surviving centuries. The experiment isolates a smaller, decisive step. More possible teachers become an advantage when a learner can select or re-examine the example that matters.

### Keep a second route open

Another experiment tested how learners were connected. Groups of six people tried to discover combinations of virtual ingredients. Some groups could see everybody else's attempts after every round. Others worked mostly in pairs, with occasional movement between pairs. The fully connected groups quickly learned from visible success, but tended to converge on one line of search. The partly connected groups kept different lines alive long enough to combine them. More than half of those groups discovered both prerequisite paths; none of the fully connected groups did.

The productive arrangement combined room to explore with later contact across groups. Too little contact can strand knowledge. Too much imitation of the first visible winner can erase alternatives before anyone knows which will be needed. The cultural record needs both faithful inheritance and a route by which another group can bring it something worth changing.

The bee's demonstration had to be legible. The plane's fold had to survive comparison. The arrowhead had to remain available when its next maker chose what to copy. The ingredient groups had to keep a second possibility alive. Counting minds leaves all four jobs unfinished.

### Two maps of one city

A crow feeding beside a bin has chosen a place to eat. It has not necessarily chosen a place to raise chicks.

In Debrecen, Hungary, researchers completed **241 surveys of sixteen city sections over three years**, recording **7,144 crow observations**. During the breeding season, more bins in the wider surrounding area went with more observed birds. Yet the probability that a section would become newly occupied for breeding was lower where bins and restaurants were more numerous. The authors proposed human disturbance and the presence of nonbreeding birds among the possible explanations.

One city held a map of visits and a map of settlement. They answered different questions. The study measured habitat use; its lesson for us is a measurement analogy. The crowded workshop and the vanished panel now give that distinction a human counterpart. Many encounters with skill do not tell us which motion reached the next apprentice’s hands. We need a map of the transfer.

### The room around the mind

Take the map room away altogether. Imagine a human newborn alone in a forest, with no caregiver, language, tools, or demonstration. The child would almost certainly die. Even keeping her alive would not supply the inherited practices she could learn in a human community. An ape infant also depends on its mother and group. A developing brain needs a world in which its capacities can become working abilities.

Researchers gave **two-and-a-half-year-old children**, chimpanzees, and orangutans tasks involving the physical and social world. The children and chimpanzees performed similarly on the physical tasks; the children did much better on the social ones. Part of the human biological gift is a remarkable capacity to learn *with* other people. What the forest image takes away is the community through which that gift acquires its contents.

An experienced driver does something strange without noticing it. The car is wider than her body, yet she approaches a narrow gap and says, “I cannot fit.” She is judging the gap through a vehicle she has learned to handle. For the duration of the drive, her practical range of movement includes the steering wheel, the car's edges, and the road she can see. Her skin has not expanded to the doors. Her working ability has expanded into a system beyond her skin.

The same thing happens when a student learns to work with a map, a diagram, or mathematical notation. Give two children the same problem. Give one a familiar diagram, a worked example, and a teacher who can show where a calculation went wrong. Give the other the question alone. If the first child succeeds, the child has done the thinking. The marks around her have changed which thoughts she can carry to the end.

The driving image has a limit. A car usually extends one driver's action in the present. A shared notation can extend the action of strangers who lived centuries apart. It can also be taught selectively, guarded, revised, or imposed. The student does not simply *have* the map; she enters a room where other people have decided which maps remain on the shelves and how to read them.

This is what an external memory ledger adds to a capable brain. It gives a person a place to start that previous people paid to reach. The marks do not contain meaning by themselves. As with the clay tablet in the previous chapter, the reader needs the learned code and the circumstances of use. But a readable record can preserve a distinction after its maker dies. An error can be marked in the margin. Two inherited solutions can be set side by side and tested.

A genius can make a move few others could make. But the move begins on terrain no individual built. The algebraic sign, the measuring instrument, the record of a failed experiment, and the teacher who explained an error all enter the next mind as possible moves. Before crediting the navigator, ask which routes she could see. Originality can be a new passage through a map room assembled by the dead.

Michael Muthukrishna, Joseph Henrich, and their colleagues carried the feedback further in the Cultural Brain Hypothesis. They modeled how access to socially learned information, the cost of maintaining a large brain, opportunities to learn from others, group structure, and the long period of human development can evolve together. A brain can make cultural learning possible; a growing store of useful culture can, in turn, change the value of a brain that can learn from it. The record shapes the organ that reads the record.

The model makes dependence run in both directions. Accumulated knowledge changes what a learner can do and which capacities are valuable to develop. The child inherits a brain and a world arranged for that brain to learn in. Each can alter the future of the other.

### Who keeps the maps?

At first, the map room sounds like a public gift. It can be. A correction attached to a recipe may save the next baker a failed loaf. A proof can let a stranger test a conclusion rather than trust the author's name. The room becomes more powerful each time an error can be found and a better version can reach the next learner.

But a map room is never a room of all possible maps. Someone chooses which book to copy, which language to teach, which maker's design to display, and which failure to leave out. A ledger can preserve a useful route and preserve a distortion with the same mechanical fidelity. Access determines who gets to correct the distortion before it becomes an inherited rule.

For each practice, follow the path from an earlier success to a later attempt. Does the learner inherit the decisive distinction? Can a failure bring back a correction? When that route breaks, discovery has to be paid for again. When it holds, a later person can spend her effort beyond the old limit.

The same architecture has a darker use. Suppose the map no longer tells a learner how to make a tool. Suppose it tells a court who owns the tool, a lender who owes the money, and a worker which company must answer for the loss. The people who keep that record can still decide which distinctions survive and which corrections arrive in time. Now the next reader's mistake is measured in cash, property, and claims against a company that may have been emptied before the claim is heard.

The map room made us powerful. The next question is who gets to own the map—and who receives the bill when its lines are drawn to someone else's advantage.
