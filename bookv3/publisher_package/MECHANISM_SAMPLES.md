# The Ledger Principle — Selected mechanism chapters

# Chapter 5: The Tollbooths

On June 7, 2024, a bankruptcy trustee issued a payment to Green Mountain Feeds. The claim number was 2. The amount Green Mountain had asked to be paid was $1,078.35. The amount the trustee's final account recognized was also $1,078.35. No reduction appeared in the *allowed* column.

The payment was $333.39.

There is a story inside those three entries: **asserted, allowed, paid**. A person can win the middle one and still lose most of the money. In this case, $744.96 of an allowed claim never reached the supplier through the bankruptcy distribution. No judge needed to declare the bill imaginary. The estate could recognize it precisely and pay only 30.92 percent.

The debtor was Central CT Coop, a Connecticut agricultural cooperative. It filed for Chapter 7 liquidation in August 2016. Green Mountain's payment went out nearly eight years later. The trustee's final account, filed that December, reported that the case had been fully administered. It lists the supplier's claim in an exhibit and records the actual payment again in a cash-disbursement register, down to the check number: 30009. This is as close as an ordinary reader can get to seeing an official right turn into cash on the same page.

The claimant has reached the correct room. The amount is recognized in full. Why is the money still missing?

### An address is not a payment

Imagine you are owed a refund by a store that has closed. You still have the receipt. The store manager agrees that the amount is correct. Then she opens the till and shows you that several people have claims on the same cash, some with rights to be paid before you. The receipt has done its job: it proves what is owed. It cannot fill the till.

Bankruptcy law formalizes that moment. A creditor files a proof of claim. Another party can object. The court may allow or disallow the claim, or allow only part of it. Then the law and the terms of any collateral determine the order in which money is distributed. A creditor can be right about the amount and still be late in the order, or face a pool too small for everyone whose claims have been allowed.

Without a collective proceeding, the quickest or best-funded creditors could empty the account. Bankruptcy can preserve value, test claims, and divide what remains by stated rules. Its strongest defense is fairness: proof, priority, and common distribution prevent a race from deciding everything.

The till had to be made. In the receipts exhibit, the cooperative's working life comes apart into sale prices: $125,000 for a 2014 hopper truck, $15,000 for a skid steer, $7,500 for propane forklifts. Other entries record collected receivables, tax refunds, and proceeds from recovery actions. Even invoices more than ninety days old contributed $116,659.45. A business that once moved feed, vehicles, and promises now had to be converted into money someone could distribute.

This is the strongest reason to pay for administration. Finding property, collecting debts, selling equipment, and pursuing recoveries can create a payment pool that no creditor could assemble alone. It is also why a large number on an old balance sheet does not answer the claimant's question. The final report needs a second number: how much was actually recovered, after the applicable rights and costs were dealt with? The trustee reported $1,658,613.82 in gross receipts. That figure describes recovered money, not the value of every asset the business had once owned.

The final account separates what each category received. More than a million dollars went to secured claims. A priority unsecured claim was paid in full. General unsecured creditors shared about fifty thousand dollars; administering the liquidation cost nearly half a million. These payments did not all draw on an identical, freely interchangeable pile: secured claims can be tied to collateral and its proceeds. But one closed case shows what different legal categories do to the same debtor's promises.

The priority unsecured claimant was a health services and insurance plan for Teamsters Local 559. Its $59,780.44 allowed claim was paid in full. Green Mountain's $1,078.35 allowed claim was paid at 30.92 percent. The difference was not that one arithmetic problem was solved correctly and the other incorrectly. The difference was the legal character of the claims and the money available at each point in the distribution. A classification became a payment rule. The page decided who absorbed the shortage.

Now turn from the category summary to the cash register. On June 7, check 30008 paid the Teamsters plan $59,780.44 and left $50,142.69 in the consolidation account. The next check, 30009, paid Green Mountain $333.39 and left $49,809.30. The remaining general unsecured distributions worked through the same class dividend, with rounding to cents, until the register reached zero.

The order of the check numbers did not create the priorities; it recorded their execution. Nor does this one account describe every earlier payment from the estate. What it supplies is the missing movement between two abstractions. The class summary says general unsecured creditors received $50,142.69. The transaction register lets us watch that sum become particular checks. The supplier's right did not vanish. The money available to satisfy the class ran out.

The report closes the account with $744.96 of Green Mountain's allowed claim unpaid. It names the creditor who bore that remainder; it supplies no account of what the supplier then had to change in its own business.

### What each booth actually does

The word *claim* makes the process sound like one question: do you have a right to be paid? In practice it is a sequence of different questions, answered by different people and records.

First is the **entry booth**. Did the creditor learn of the case, identify the debtor, and file the required proof in the required way and time? The court's final account lists many scheduled creditors marked “N/F”: no proof of claim filed. A name on a debtor's old list is not the same entry as a filed claim eligible for distribution. We cannot tell from that mark alone why any particular creditor did not file. The important fact is that the record distinguishes the two.

Second is the **recognition booth**. Was the filed amount allowed? Under federal bankruptcy law, a filed proof is generally deemed allowed unless a party in interest objects; an objection can bring the amount and legal basis before the court. Central CT Coop's final account separates the dollars creditors *asserted* from the dollars *allowed*. For general unsecured claims, it reports $1,490,398.95 asserted and $162,184.18 allowed. A total cannot tell us which claims were amended, duplicated, compromised, disallowed, or reclassified. It tells us which column we have: $1.49 million asserted is a different fact from $162,184.18 allowed.

Green Mountain passed that booth intact. The whole $1,078.35 was allowed.

Third is the **rank booth**. What kind of claim has been allowed? Secured creditors have rights against specified collateral. Certain unsecured claims receive statutory priority. General unsecured claims come behind higher-ranking claims and the costs of administering the estate. The hierarchy can be defended as the price of lending against collateral, protecting particular vulnerable claims, and funding a process that collects and distributes assets for everyone. Yet the hierarchy is also a record of bargaining power and political choice. A creditor who can demand collateral before lending may buy a better place in line. A small supplier usually sells on terms already written by someone else.

Fourth is the **collection booth**. After the claim is recognized and ranked, what value can actually be reached? An empty debtor does not become solvent because an order says “allowed.” A trust, guarantor, insurance policy, lien, or recovery action may enlarge the reachable pool. Without an enforceable bridge, an asset held by some related but separate company is not automatically available. In the last chapter, LTL's funding agreement showed why the phrase “liability company with no assets” could be false. Here the question is the same in a more ordinary setting: what money, from which legal source, can make the final entry payable?

Finally comes **execution**. A distribution has to be made, not merely proposed. The distinction can seem pedantic until the register changes. Check 30010 initially recorded $1,257.67 for PC Tech LLC on June 7. In October the claim was transferred. On October 21, the trustee reversed the original check, putting $1,257.67 back into the account, then issued check 30026 for the same amount to Andrew Shawn Donaldson, the transferee. The account returned to zero.

Count both positive entries and you invent a second payment. Read only the June payee and you miss who received the replacement. Read only the final claims exhibit and you see Donaldson's name without the earlier route. The reversal and replacement make the history legible: one claim, one distribution amount, a changed recipient. A useful record retains the correction that explains its present state.

For Green Mountain, the report records the June distribution without such a reversal. The trustee certified that funds on hand had been distributed and that bank statements, deposit slips, and canceled checks had been submitted to the United States Trustee. The filed report is our payment evidence; its claim-allowance line alone would not have supplied it.

Each booth can stop or shrink the route. It can also screen a false claim, protect a priority, or preserve a common pool. Challenging its decision can change the route. A right becomes a remedy only after the record has carried it through every necessary stage.

### The multiplication a small claimant feels

Suppose 100 creditors each have a $100 claim. At the first hurdle, 70 percent pass. At each of the next three, 70 percent of those who reached it pass. The arithmetic is 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 × 0.7 = 0.2401: about 24 claims reach the far side. Pay those claims in full and the original group receives about $24 per $100 claimed on average. No single hurdle needs to look severe for the compound effect to be severe.

The image counts passage through an invented system. Its 70 percent is the share passing each hurdle among those who reached it. In real cases, a missing document can affect several decisions; an amendment can reopen a route. We would have to follow the same claims to measure the passage rates. Green Mountain's 30.92 percent came from the distribution available to its class after its claim was allowed in full.

The question is **which decision or shortage took the money between one column and the next?** In the Connecticut report, recognition did not reduce Green Mountain's claim. The reduction happened between allowance and distribution. Improving only the ease of filing would not have supplied the missing $744.96. To change that result, one would have to change the assets recovered, the claims ahead of it, the legal ranking, or the applicable source of payment. A reform aimed at the wrong booth may produce more beautifully recorded rights and the same small checks.

Keep three questions beside the report: **Did the claim pass recognition? What resources could pay it? What share was paid?**

Give the mechanisms short names: *psi* (ψ) for passage through recognition, *K* for the ceiling imposed by reachable resources, and *phi* (φ) for the share paid. The symbols keep those questions apart. Ask for the filed amount, the allowed amount, and the paid amount. Each column needs the next before it can tell you what the creditor recovered.

Now improve every hurdle in the invented system from 70 to 90 percent. About 66 of the original 100 claims reach the end. Suppose the creditors who pass must divide only $1,000, with no claims ahead of them or additional costs. Whether 24 or 66 claims arrive, their total payment cannot exceed $1,000. Better gates recognize more rights; they do not refill the pool.

A successful clawback, guarantee, insurance claim, or another enforceable source could raise *K*. Imagine a genuine $100 claim with all procedures cleared and only $10 reachable for it. An easier form cannot produce the eleventh dollar. There are two handles: change what can pass, and change what can pay. A reform needs to reach the handle responsible for the shortage.

### A pattern bigger than one cooperative

Does the same separation hold beyond a small cooperative? We turned to the Florida–UCLA–LoPucki Bankruptcy Research Database of large public-company bankruptcies. Its January 2023 release contains 1,218 cases. Our calculation uses the subset for which both secured and general unsecured class claims and distributions are recorded in the same case: 105 cases, most from Chapter 11 rather than the Connecticut cooperative's Chapter 7.

In that matched subset, the median secured class recovered the full recorded claim. The median general unsecured class recovered 20 cents per dollar. Secured recovery exceeded unsecured recovery in 95 of the 105 cases. These large cases have their own procedures and distributions. Alongside the cooperative, they show why the category of an allowed claim matters: legal rank helps decide how much of the recognized amount reaches the payment column.

A second kind of record points in the same direction. Moody's study of defaulted debt found lower trading-price recoveries as bond claims moved down the seniority ladder: a mean of 57.4 cents per dollar of face value for senior secured debt versus 28.9 cents for junior subordinated debt in its 1982–2004 recovery observations. Those are market prices around default, not the final checks from Central CT Coop, and the people holding those instruments may have bought them at different prices. Priority was valuable enough for markets to price it before final payment arrived.

Perhaps those prices reflected panic rather than what creditors ultimately recovered. Moody's also followed claims through resolution. Its 2007 Ultimate Recovery Database report gives average discounted recoveries of 82 percent for bank loans, 65 percent for senior secured bonds, 38 percent for senior unsecured bonds, and 15 percent for junior subordinated bonds. The bank-loan median was 100 percent. The hierarchy survived into the value creditors received at resolution.

These recoveries could include cash or replacement securities. Moody's valued them and discounted them back to the last interest payment using the instrument's pre-default coupon rate. That time adjustment matters. A dollar arriving years later cannot be treated as the same investment result as a dollar arriving now. The price at default, the value at resolution, and the trustee's actual check are distinct records pointing toward the same importance of position in the queue.

The strongest defense still has force: scarce assets need a hierarchy and valid claims need proof. A secured lender may have supplied funding that kept the business open for years. A priority health plan may be protecting workers. A trustee's fees may be the cost of turning scattered property into any payment at all. The question this chapter asks is more exacting: **who had a chance to set the categories and prices before the shortage, and who encountered them only when the bill came due?** A system can distribute scarce money according to its rules and still make the weaker party carry a risk that was never visible in the price they received.

Return to claim 2. Green Mountain's amount was fully allowed. The next column paid 30.92 percent and left 69.08 percent with the creditor. The official record acknowledged the obligation without making the supplier whole. Recognition named the bearer of the shortage.

### The amount no one can make vanish

The shortfall can stay with a supplier as an unpaid receivable. It can sit with a pension, a lender, an insurer, or a government. Before the outcome is resolved, an account may still carry a larger value than will eventually be recovered. The difference waits for a decision about who will absorb it.

Green Mountain's $744.96 has a final address in the report: the creditor. Other losses stay in the system for years while the records continue to say that loans are performing and capital is sound. The account remains open. When that accumulated gap finally demands payment, who gets the check—and who gets the bill?

# Chapter 10: The Language Between Machines

Three objects sit on a laboratory table. Each has the same shape. Each appears alone. One is orange, one green, one blue.

A speaker gives all three the same name: `wipisu`.

The speaker is a language model, and the table is a list of simulated objects, but the mistake is recognizable. Imagine a shipping clerk who has learned to label every box by size and weight while dropping the label that says what is inside. Most packages still reach the right shelf. The clerk becomes very fast. Then a dangerous package arrives whose color was the warning.

In the experiment, nobody shipped a dangerous package. The objects were combinations of shape, color, and amount. There were twenty-seven possible combinations, and the researchers examined the artificial words that language models made for them. The same word appearing against those three colors is visible in their published table. It is one small failure inside a larger success: the models had discovered a way to reuse parts of words. In the same vocabulary, one ending tended to mark *one*, another *two*, and another *three*. A model could use the pattern to name objects it had never seen.

This example came from a code shaped by interaction within one generation. It had learned to reuse parts, and it had already begun to forget color. When the researchers then passed related vocabularies through generations of model learners, the codes became easier to inherit while growing more ambiguous.

That is the peril hidden in the word *efficient*. A good shorthand removes what a partner can already infer. A bad shorthand removes what neither partner has yet been made to care about. Both can look like progress on a scoreboard that counts only the distinctions tested today.

The last chapter's checker could not read the record it was meant to inspect. Here the machines can read their shared record well enough to pass it on. The unsettling question is what happens to a fact when the code they inherit stops carrying it.

### The game before the language

Go back to a simpler laboratory. One agent sees a target. Another sees several candidates. The first may send a short message; the second must choose the right candidate. A correct choice earns a reward. The two agents begin without an agreed word for *blue*, *triangle*, or *three*. They may settle on any signals that help them win.

If two coworkers invent `B2` to mean “the second blue box,” they are doing something similar. The symbol has no natural connection to the object. It works because one coworker uses it consistently and the other acts on it. If the convention is written down, a third coworker can learn it. The marks become a little external memory for the pair.

There is a temptation to look at successful coordination and announce that the agents have invented language in the full human sense. In 2017, Satwik Kottur and colleagues tested that leap. Their agents often achieved nearly perfect task rewards using invented messages that were difficult for humans to interpret and did not consistently break into reusable parts. A private code could solve the game without becoming a neat miniature of English. When the researchers imposed restrictions on what the agents could say and remember, more human-readable, compositional patterns could be coaxed out.

This was the first failed walk: *if agents need to communicate, surely a clear language will emerge*. They needed to coordinate. They did not need to make their messages clear to the person standing outside the game.

Consider an office where two experienced workers have handled the same machine for years. One taps the housing twice; the other changes a setting. Their signal is perfectly useful to the pair. A newly hired safety inspector may understand neither the tap nor the setting. It would be foolish to call the tap meaningless merely because the inspector is lost. It would be equally foolish to say the inspector has oversight because the tap was logged.

The comparison stops at a point that matters. Human workers can often stop and explain what a tap meant. A trained agent's convention may be distributed across weights, task history, and its partner's responses. A researcher can sometimes reconstruct a mapping by intervening and testing alternatives, but there may be no complete dictionary already sitting inside the system, waiting to be printed. The game measures coordination and aspects of structure; it does not measure a machine's intention to conceal anything.

The record, here, is not a secret diary. It is the durable mapping between a signal, a situation, and the next move. Two agents can maintain that mapping through use even when no human has named its rules. Once new agents inherit the mapping, the code begins to have a history beyond either original partner.

### Send in the new hire

Fushan Li and Michael Bowling changed one condition of the game: they replaced the listener.

Their speaker could send two symbols selected from a small vocabulary. The listener chose one target from five candidates. The objects varied in color and shape. Under one training schedule, the speaker and the same listener practiced together for 300,000 iterations. Under another, a new listener replaced the old one every 6,000 iterations. Fifty listeners passed through the speaker's working life.

Both schedules eventually produced communication that was about 97 percent successful when evaluated with their trained partners. If the researchers had stopped at the familiar accuracy score, the two histories would have looked nearly alike. But the reset speaker had learned a different kind of message. When a fresh listener arrived, it learned that speaker's code more quickly. The code had also become more structured: similar objects tended to receive more similar messages. The no-reset pair could win together without paying the cost of teaching a stranger.

The new hire changed what survived.

Think of the difference between a note that says “do the Tuesday thing” and one that says “when the pressure rises, open the left valve.” The first is shorter for a veteran who remembers Tuesday. The second travels to a worker hired on Wednesday. Repeated turnover makes the veteran pay for ambiguity at every handoff. Ease of teaching becomes part of the selection pressure on the code.

There is no mystical impulse toward clarity inside the speaker. The rules of the workplace changed. The easiest code to maintain with one partner was no longer the easiest code to maintain across a succession of partners. The experiment shows that replacement can select for teachable structure without a human writing the words. It does not show that the newcomers were less capable models, or that the resulting signals became transparent to any outsider who had not learned the game.

The reset closes one escape: a private code does not remain private merely because its speakers have never met a stranger. Newcomers can learn it. Yet the newcomers in this experiment trained inside the game; an outside supervisor did not inherit their training history. Transmission and auditability have to be tested separately.

Our own fable from two chapters ago worked by a related route. The lion, the adviser, and the bull are easy to teach because their relations can be reconstructed in many settings. A machine code can also become portable. Yet portability alone does not tell us which part of the world the code preserves. The fable can travel while one reader forgets the bull's innocence. A machine vocabulary can travel while its word for the object's color disappears.

### The word that lost a color

Tom Kouwenhoven, Max Peeperkorn, and Tessa Verhoef put large language models into a version of the inheritance game. Their objects combined three shapes, three colors, and one, two, or three copies: twenty-seven possibilities. The models saw examples for fifteen. They used artificial words made from syllables such as `su`, `pepi`, and `wipi`; later the researchers tested whether they could extend the vocabulary to the twelve combinations held back.

During communication, pairs of Llama 3 70B models took turns describing and identifying targets among distractors. Then the researchers arranged six transmission chains, each eight generations long. A later model received part of a predecessor's signal-to-object vocabulary and learned from it. The researchers selected the vocabulary with the highest measured structural similarity to pass to the next generation. The inheritance was experimental and curated, not a spontaneous migration of deployed agents from one system to another.

Something remarkable happened before the generations even began. In the first communication round, the models identified targets in roughly seven out of ten interactions, against the authors’ stated chance benchmark of one in four. Interaction helped shape parts of the initially arbitrary vocabulary into reusable elements. In one example, a word ending marked quantity while a beginning marked shape. A model could extend that pattern to an object it had not seen during teaching.

Now follow the code as it is handed down. Later models reproduced inherited signals more faithfully. The words became easier for them to learn. If the only question were “Will the next model speak this code?” the answer improved across generations.

But the second question was “What can this code still say?” In the published analysis, the proportion of uniquely named objects fell from about seven in ten in the first generation to about five in ten in the last. Signals grew longer and more ambiguous. The authors found no corresponding increase in successful communication across generations. Some lineages produced the same signal for different objects.

That is where `wipisu` returns. The table's three differently colored, otherwise matching objects share the word. If the listener's current candidates make color irrelevant, it can still pick correctly. If two of those objects appear together and color is the only difference, the word has lost the discriminating bit. This particular word came from a within-generation test, before the eight-generation chains. The chains separately show how later learners favored an easier-to-repeat but increasingly ambiguous vocabulary. Together, the observations expose two points at which a distinction can disappear. The experiment does not say that all generated codes ignored color or that every trial failed.

Look closer at how the machines were told whether a word worked. Alongside each earlier object and signal, the researchers placed a success flag: one if the listener had chosen correctly, zero if it had not. The next speaker inherited those marks as part of its context. The experiment already had a little ledger of consequences.

The published speaker prompt makes the problem visible. Its examples attach `wipisu` to the same shape and amount in three colors. The green and orange entries carry failure marks; the blue entry carries a success. This is the paper’s illustration of the prompt format, rather than a complete history of one trial. It shows what the feedback could say: this use worked; those uses did not. It could not make one shared word specify three different colors.

Think of a spare key that happens to open three lockers. A worker sent to collect a coat may succeed if only one locker contains a coat. A tick beside that errand records a successful collection. It does not certify that the key distinguishes the lockers. Put three coats behind those doors, and the missing distinction becomes the entire job. The machine word has the same dependence on the alternatives its listener faces. A successful choice can give an ambiguous signal credit.

The surviving record must therefore carry enough to reconstruct the test: what was requested, what alternatives were available, what signal was sent, and which choice followed. A bare success mark compresses that history into reassurance. The locker is an analogy; the prompt’s success flags and repeated word are the actual experimental design.

These were textual objects in a controlled game. The shipment is our bridge into the result, not an event the researchers observed. The question it gives us is real: when a consequential category is seldom tested, will the next version of a code preserve the distinction on which a later person depends?

The handoff introduced another filter. Researchers chose the vocabulary whose similar meanings received the most similar words. That rewards a useful kind of pattern. But a perfectly orderly label for shape and quantity can still omit color. A successor can reproduce that pattern faithfully while inheriting its blind spot. Two tests are needed: can the new learner recover the convention, and can that convention distinguish the cases placed before it? Improving the first score cannot answer the second question.

The researchers themselves supply the important counterweight. With enough pressure to distinguish meanings during actual communication, degenerate vocabularies may be checked. In this setup, the pull toward being easy for a model to learn was stronger than the pressure to keep every object distinct. The record kept being copied. What it could distinguish did not automatically keep up.

### The four comforting explanations

The first says that machine codes are gibberish. Gibberish would not help a listener pick targets beyond chance, allow a new listener to learn faster after resets, or let an unseen object inherit a structured name. These systems carried usable relations. The fact that a human needs experiments to recover them does not make the relations imaginary.

The second says that every effective code can be translated back into ordinary language, so no oversight problem remains. Some can be mapped after systematic probing. Indeed, the `wipisu` table is already a partial translation. But a successful translation requires access to the inputs, the signals, the partners, and the decisions that made a signal useful. Even then, a dictionary made for yesterday's code can lag behind a new generation's changes. What matters is whether the human can check a consequential distinction *before* the decision uses it, not whether a researcher can explain a pattern after collecting the full experiment.

The third says that if newcomers can learn the code, humans can learn it just as cheaply. Li and Bowling's newcomers were trained listeners in a narrow game. Kouwenhoven's successors were language models given a structured list of examples and selected vocabularies. A human supervisor can be taught a stable convention, but training a human on one code is a different operation from understanding a changing, task-specific code at every handoff. The newcomer result establishes inheritance. It does not grant outsiders immediate fluency.

The fourth says that the simplest cure is to require every message to be plain English. There is a cost worth naming. In one cooperative navigation simulation, agents using a learned signaling protocol reached their goal in an average of 28.7 steps near the end of training; agents constrained to a fixed, human-designed symbolic protocol took 43.2. The latter protocol was a simple rule for signals, not full English. It is evidence that *this particular imposed code* carried a performance penalty in *this particular task*, not a theorem that readable communication always fails.

The strongest reply to that fourth explanation comes from a hybrid protocol called HyLaT. It lets agents exchange dense latent information while making selected answers and commitments in text.

In the authors’ reported comparison, the full-text system averaged 505.03 communication tokens and 5.47 seconds; HyLaT averaged 72.01 tokens and 1.47 seconds, with close aggregate task accuracy.

This is an engineered way to keep a readable surface without forcing every intermediate message through it. It leaves a sharper question: **who decides which facts must emerge from the dense channel?** If the selection rule lets color vanish, a grammatical final answer can still name the wrong box.

Mycal Tucker and colleagues attacked the code's incentives from another direction. They trained agents while balancing task utility, how much the signal revealed, and how complicated the vocabulary became. Paying attention to those pressures helped produce compact lexicons closer in size to human ones without surrendering the task. Lexicon size is not an audit of the message's truth. It is evidence that the shape of machine communication responds to what designers make costly and what they reward.

**A code can win, travel, and be summarized in fluent language while leaving a consequential distinction unaccounted for.** Kottur separates success from readable structure. Li and Bowling separate success from ease of teaching. Kouwenhoven separates ease of teaching from retained distinctions. The hybrid design separates a readable final answer from a readable intermediate exchange. Each experiment removes one place where a supervisor might have assumed the work was done.

### Read the consequence, not just the message

We can make oversight more concrete than “understand everything the agents say.” Imagine a pair of machine workers exchanging a signal before one authorizes a payment. A human auditor may not be able to translate the entire signal stream. She can still require the record of what claim was presented, which source was consulted, what rule authorized the payment, the amount sent, and the account that received it. She can ask whether changing a disputed input changes the decision. She can compare a true source with a shuffled one to test whether the purported check had causal force.

That is not a promise that receipts solve every governance problem. A receipt can record a bad decision accurately. A hash can prove that a message was not altered without revealing whether the message was fair. A model can produce a polished textual explanation after acting for reasons the explanation does not capture. The witness has to connect source, code, action, and correction. Chapter 9 showed what happens when even the first connection is miswired.

Nor can we move every burden onto the person downstream. A patient should not need to learn an agent's dialect before challenging a denial of care. A worker should not need to reconstruct a classifier's training lineage before contesting a lost wage. If institutions choose the faster code, they inherit the duty to preserve the decision-relevant distinctions and make a challenge possible while it can still matter. The cost of translation belongs with the operator who gains from compression.

The successor inherits the word. Will it inherit the distinction the next task demands?

`Wipisu` is memorable because it names a success and a failure at once. The code learned to carry shape and amount. Its reader could sometimes act well. Three colors went by under one name. The later transmission chains add the unsettling possibility: a vocabulary can become easier to hand forward while preserving fewer distinctions.

What if the next generation learns not only that word, but a whole world described in such words? What happens when its training record is made increasingly of the previous generation's descriptions, and the missing colors are no longer present in the examples against which the code is tested? An absent distinction can begin to look as though it never existed.
