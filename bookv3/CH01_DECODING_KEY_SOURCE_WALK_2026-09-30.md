# Chapter 1 — The lost reader and the surviving mark

The new Rosetta Stone passage answers a question raised by the Susa envelope and the Chinese-character anchor: what happens when a record survives but the practice of reading it does not?

The [British Museum's object record](https://www.britishmuseum.org/collection/object/Y_EA24) describes the 196 BCE stela as a priestly decree concerning Ptolemy V, written in **three scripts**, hieroglyphic, demotic, and Greek. Its [curatorial explanation](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/everything-you-ever-wanted-know-about-rosetta-stone) says hieroglyphic literacy disappeared after the script ceased to be used around the end of the fourth century AD; the broken stone was found in 1799; scholars could read Greek; Young identified phonetic signs in Ptolemy's name; and Champollion used Coptic and other texts to extend the reading, announcing the breakthrough in 1822. The same source makes clear that the inscription was no automatic dictionary. The museum's [decipherment account](https://www.britishmuseum.org/blog/eureka-finding-key-ancient-egypt) further identifies the earlier scholars and the mistaken idea that the Egyptian signs represented only things or ideas.

**Literary function:** The familiar bilingual-recipe analogy gives readers a decoding key; the Young/Champollion sequence gives the failed walk and repair. The analogy ends where the scripts cease to line up word for word. The inference is about a record's dependence on a living interpretive route, not a claim that the stone itself performed the decipherment or that one individual invented the entire route.
