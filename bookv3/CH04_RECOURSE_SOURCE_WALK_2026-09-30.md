## LTL: a separate company with a valuable payment right

The divisional merger occurred October 12, 2021; LTL filed October 14. The funding right made J&J and New Consumer jointly and severally responsible for qualifying payments, with few conditions and no repayment obligation. Its floor was tied to New Consumer's value at the merger, estimated at roughly $61.5 billion. Outside bankruptcy the permitted uses included talc-related costs and normal expenses. Inside bankruptcy they included administrative expenses and funding a claimant trust. The Third Circuit stressed both the value of the right and the strong credit quality of the payers in rejecting LTL's asserted financial distress. The amount is a contractual right described by the court, not cash already transferred to claimants.

The chapter's three-question translation follows the contract: who can demand; who must supply; whether the payment must be repaid. A corporate chart or cash balance alone does not answer those questions. This strengthens the argument that effective reach depends on enforceable passages.

## Bestwall: the same bridge, a different procedural consequence

Bestwall owed indemnification to New GP; New GP owed qualifying funding to Bestwall when relevant resources were insufficient. The majority's footnote 13 explains why a judgment against New GP could affect Bestwall's estate, including when New GP supplied the funds for Bestwall's indemnification. Bestwall's own available resources came first. The dissent argued that the indemnity-and-funding loop was circular and challenged the use of constructed relationships to establish bankruptcy jurisdiction. The chapter reconstructs a possible payment to show the competing readings. It does not report such a payment as an observed transaction.

The majority affirmed the preliminary injunction in 2023. That order restricted separate suits against New GP; it did not determine final individual recoveries. The chapter retains the separate 2025 jurisdictional-dismissal appeal and its distinct disposition.

## The reader's three shortcuts

Big Lots tests whether wealth somewhere in a corporate group automatically backs a note issued by one member. LTL tests whether a separate liability company is necessarily empty. Bestwall and Red River test whether available funding alone preserves a claimant's forum and voice. Each shortcut fails against its own record. The resulting question is concrete: who can compel which payer to provide money, on what conditions, through which procedure?
