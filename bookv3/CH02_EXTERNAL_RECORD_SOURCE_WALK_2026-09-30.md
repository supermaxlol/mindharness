## The conflicting walks

The [2013 Derex experiment](https://www.nature.com/articles/nature12774) found benefits of larger groups in a computer game with an arrowhead and a harder fishing-net task. Its published abstract and extended figures show that the large-group effect was task dependent: sixteen-person groups improved a simple artifact, while the eight-person groups did better than the sixteen-person groups at the end of the harder net task because early alternatives could obscure a pivotal demonstration. The full article is subscription content in this source walk, so the main text does not reconstruct private interface steps from it.

The [2019 Fay paper-plane experiment](https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC6452720/) already supplies the opposing result in Chapter 2: a bigger set of examples offered better available designs, yet performance did not accumulate across generations when learners saw two or four predecessors. The new 2026 paper directly tests a mechanism that can reconcile the laboratory results.

## The experiment the chapter now follows

The authors recruited 287 participants for Experiment 1 and 654 for Experiment 2, **941 total**; 281 and 644, **925 total**, consented to data use and were analyzed. Participants repeatedly drew virtual arrowheads, received scores, and, in social-learning conditions, saw earlier designs from three- or six-person groups. The comparison in the new prose comes from **Experiment 2**. All models were viewed in sequence. In the External Record condition, the viewed arrowheads, scores, and width values remained on screen during the next design attempt; in the View All condition they did not. The box was cleared for the following observation phase.

Across the six-person conditions, performance improved over trials with the record (p < .001) and did not show improvement without it (p = .421). Six-person groups outperformed three-person groups with the record (p = .034); the all-models condition gave no statistical evidence of a size effect (p = .178). The record condition produced stronger payoff-biased copying of high-ranked models. The authors did **not** directly measure recombination. Their exploratory comparison suggests the effect may chiefly reflect attention filtering.

Experiment 1 tested that rival separately. When a participant could choose one model to inspect, a six-person advantage appeared (p = .037); requiring all models yielded no statistical evidence of that advantage (p = .499). Thus an external record is an experimentally supported route to useful selection in this task, not the only route. A live teacher, a filter, or a well-designed memory aid can also alter what a learner carries into the next attempt.

**Metaphor boundary:** The on-screen aid survived one production phase, not generations. The paper establishes a controlled step between access to more examples and cumulative improvement. The book's wider claim about archives across centuries rests on the archaeological, historical, and other cultural evidence in Chapters 1–2; this single experiment does not prove it alone.
