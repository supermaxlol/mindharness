## Primary record

Monti et al., *Willful Modulation of Brain Activity in Disorders of Consciousness*, NEJM 362:579–589 (2010), DOI https://doi.org/10.1056/NEJMoa0905370. Full author-lab copy checked live: https://montilab.psych.ucla.edu/wp-content/uploads/sites/49/2015/11/Monti_etal_2010_NEJM.pdf. The first Owen-lab PDF request failed; the UCLA author-lab copy supplied the full text. Publisher metadata/search text and PubMed 20130250 corroborated the record.

Methods, p. 581: localizer sessions establish the two imagery signatures. Participants use one for yes and the other for no; communication cues use the neutral word answer. Questions were chosen so investigators would not know the correct answer before interpreting imaging. All sixteen healthy controls and one patient underwent communication scans; the one patient had six questions. Do not transfer six-question performance to all 54 patients or all five responders.

Results, p. 585 and Figure 3/p. 587: Patient 23 gives the correct affirmative motor-imagery response to Alexander and the correct negative spatial-imagery response to Thomas. Figure caption explicitly states that names were changed for privacy. Five of six responses matched correct answers. Sixth scan was undecodable because usable activity was absent in the designated regions, not because the wrong answer pattern appeared.

Methods and discussion, pp. 581, 585: earlier vegetative-state diagnoses were followed by further bedside assessment indicating minimally conscious status; reliable bedside communication was still unavailable. The male communicating patient is distinct from the female 2006 patient. The paper cannot establish whether awareness was present during all earlier assessments or had subsequently recovered.

## Alternatives and the surviving argument

Automatic response to a cue is challenged by the neutral answer cue and question-sensitive selection between established imagery signatures. An interpretation tailored to the expected factual answer is addressed by decoding before factual answers were known. Treating a silent scan as no is rejected by the sixth scan’s actual status: no answer could be recovered. The paper leaves several reasons for that nonresponse unresolved.

The form’s third entry is the book’s explanatory analogy and operational inference. No actual institutional form, treatment decision, patient preference about care, or clinical recommendation is invented. The account establishes verified communication for this patient in this experiment. It does not settle a complete theory of experience or generalize patient capacity to a fluent machine.
